99=* Hagino Top Lady of the "Glamorous Clan"\nMyrtle was a broodmare imported from Britain in 1957. Thanks to her offspring Oaks winner Yamapit and two-time champion mare Itto, her family was dubbed the "Glamorous Clan" after a popular novel of the time.\nItto's first foal, Hagino Top Lady, made a dazzling debut, setting a Japanese record of 57.2 seconds for 1000m on turf. It stood as Hakodate's 2yo record for over 45 years until 2025.\nIn 1980, at 3, she ran in the Oka Sho in just her third career start. Against rivals like previous champion 2yo filly Laughontes, she set a furious pace from the start and never gave up the lead. No other horse won the Oka Sho in its third start until Daring Tact in 2020. She later won the Queen Elizabeth II Cup too, was named champion 3yo filly, and raised the "Glamorous Clan's" fame.\n\n* Birth of a White Horse\nWhite horses command attention with their beauty. Japanese racing first recognized them around this time.\nIn 1979 a pure white foal, Hakutaiyu, was born on a farm in Urakawa, Hokkaido. Grey horses, also white-coated, aren't born white (they whiten with age), and neither of his parents was grey, so he couldn't be grey. Some suspected albinism, since a pure white horse had never been seen in Japan, but as white racehorses already ran abroad, he was registered as Japan's first white thoroughbred.\nIn 1980 Japan's first "Coat Color Council" recognized "white" as the 8th coat color, with Hakutaiyu as No. 1. Sports papers and TV covered him, and as "Hakuba-kun" he was loved far beyond racing - laying the groundwork for the white horses' popularity that continues today.
100=* Breeding Horses That Can Compete With the World\nCompared with Britain, birthplace of modern racing, and other countries, Japanese racing started late - a developing racing nation. Rules limiting foreign runners and burdensome quarantine made it so closed to foreign horses that it was mocked as "isolationist racing."\nCalls grew to "breed horses that can compete with the world," and in 1981 Japan's first international invitational race, the Japan Cup, was finally held.\nSeven foreign horses came from America, Canada and India. Japan's defenders included Hoyo Boy, winner of the Arima Kinen and Tenno Sho (Autumn), and Monte Prince, the uncrowned prince twice 2nd in classics. But foreign horses took 1st to 4th - a rout. The winner, American mare Mairzy Doates, hadn't even won a G1-level race at home, and she broke the course record by a full second. Japanese racing was shown just how high the world's wall was.\n\n* Tenno Sho "Win and Out" Rule Abolished\nThe Tenno Sho has been run twice a year, spring and autumn, since the first running in 1937 (then the Teishitsu Gosho Ten). It used a "win and out" rule: a horse that won could never run in it again - reportedly so that a past winner losing wouldn't hurt the race's prestige. But as a result, the autumn field was sometimes weaker than the spring one.\nPerhaps for that reason, the rule was finally abolished this year. Naturally horses then tried to win it twice, but in 1984 the autumn race was cut from 3200m to 2000m, making a spring-autumn double very hard. Only 6 horses have ever done it (same year: Tamamo Cross, Special Week, T.M. Opera O, Meisho Samson, Kitasan Black; across years: Super Creek).
101=* Mejiro Titan: Father and Son Tenno Sho Winners\nMejiro Asama, autumn 1970 Tenno Sho winner, disproved the belief that "Partholon's offspring are precocious milers," and went to stud in 1973 with a syndicate and high hopes. But in his first year he covered 28 mares and none conceived, leaving his owners in despair. They kept trying, and in his 5th year at stud came Mejiro Titan.\nMejiro Titan debuted at 3. It took 4 starts to win, but he improved rapidly and won the St. Lite Kinen, a graded race, in September. In 1982, at 4, he went for a father-son double in the Tenno Sho (Autumn). He launched a long run from the third turn of the second lap, led in the straight and held on, completing the double in Japanese record time, a full second faster than last year's winner Hoyo Boy.\nHe was first trained by Tokichi Ogata, called "the Great Ogata" for his huge contributions to racing. Tokichi, who had also trained his sire Mejiro Asama, rated Mejiro Titan highly, but died just before the St. Lite Kinen. His son Moritsugu took over, and together he and Mejiro Titan won the Tenno Sho as if carrying on their fathers' wishes - a win built on the bond between man and horse.\n\n* Northern Taste, Japan's Greatest Sire\nNorthern Dancer won two legs of the US Triple Crown and became a world-famous sire. His son Northern Taste was bought at an American sale by the Shadai Group. He raced in France and Britain with a respectable record, including the G1 Prix de la Foret. He then went to stud in Japan, though some breeders doubted him because of his big head and short legs.\nBut once his offspring raced, opinions changed completely. His first crop included Arima Kinen winner Amber Shadai, his runners did brilliantly, and in 1982 he became leading sire. His offspring kept showing such development that people said "Northern Taste's runners change three times," and he was leading sire 10 times in all (JRA and local combined).\nHis sire line has since died out in Japan, but he undoubtedly contributed hugely to Japanese racing - truly "the horse who changed Japanese breeding."
102=* Mr. C.B., Flawless and Free\nTosho Boy, "Pegasus." C.B. Queen, who won the Mainichi Okan in record time. Two outstanding horses who, by a strange twist of fate, debuted in the same race. Their son was Mr. C.B. (C.B. Queen's first foal).\nThe first classic, the Satsuki Sho, was run on heavy ground, seemingly bad for a closer like him, but he charged boldly from last to win. In the Japanese Derby he ignored the jinx that "you can't win unless you're in the top 10 at the first turn," closing from third-last to win.\nIn the Kikuka Sho, going for the Triple Crown, he again raced at the back, but moved on the uphill of the third turn and passed the leaders. Then, on Kyoto's downhill, where the rule is to go easy, he accelerated against all convention, stunning the crowd. He took the lead at the final turn and won with no one near - the third Triple Crown winner ever and the first in 19 years, since Shinzan. He was also the first Triple Crown winner by a Japanese-bred sire.\n\n* Farewell, Shuji Terayama\nPoet Shuji Terayama, known as "the alchemist of words," loved racing so much he became an owner, and is known for the memorial poem "Farewell, Ten Point" he wrote when Ten Point died.\nAmong jockeys he favored Masato Yoshinaga, known for the extreme "Yoshinaga Special" tactics, often writing about him and vowing to "keep writing until he wins the Derby." But Terayama died suddenly this year. Yoshinaga won the Japanese Derby with Mr. C.B. about three weeks later.\nTerayama's book "Uma Yabure te Sogen Ari" contains this line: "I don't necessarily think racing is a metaphor for life. On the contrary, I think life is a metaphor for racing." It shows his own racing philosophy: those who watch racing are what matter.
103=* The Unbeaten Triple Crown\nIn the year the JRA introduced graded races, ranking 15 races as G1, a historic first was achieved.\nSymboli Rudolf was rated highly even before his debut. When trainer Yuji Nohira asked jockey Yukio Okabe to ride him, he said, "If you ever find a horse stronger than Rudolf, you can switch anytime." As if to prove that confidence wasn't overblown, Rudolf won his first 3 starts with Okabe.\nIn 1984 he took on the classics and won the Satsuki Sho clearly. At the ceremony Okabe raised one finger high - "first crown." In the Japanese Derby Rudolf ignored his rider's cues and ran his own race to win, and Okabe raised two fingers. Afterwards he said, "Today Rudolf taught me." After the Derby his camp planned a trip abroad for the Arc, but a shoulder injury scrapped it, and he stayed in the classics. In the Kikuka Sho he showed the "Emperor's" style, bursting clear in the straight to become Japan's first unbeaten Triple Crown winner. At last Okabe thrust three fingers to the sky.\nOne can't help imagining how he'd have run in the Arc - but had he gone, there might have been no unbeaten Triple Crown.\n\n* A Japan Cup Clash of Triple Crown Winners, and a Shock Ending\nIn the three years since the Japan Cup was founded under the slogan "horses that can compete with the world," Japanese horses kept losing to foreigners. But the year before, Kyoei Promise finished 2nd, and fans felt victory was within reach.\nIn October 1984 Tokyo Racecourse installed "Turf Vision," the JRA's first giant screen, changing the old style of watching through binoculars. Then last year's Triple Crown winner Mr. C.B. and Symboli Rudolf, unbeaten Triple Crown winner just two weeks earlier, both entered. Everyone focused on the first-ever clash of Triple Crown winners and how they'd take on world-class foreign horses. Early on, as the others watched each other, Takarazuka Kinen winner Katsuragi Ace went for broke, opening a lead of over 10 lengths. At the final turn it shrank to about 3 lengths, and Rudolf and the foreigners closed in the straight, but Katsuragi Ace, who had saved plenty at the slow pace, found a second wind and held on to win as 10th choice.\nIn its 4th year, Japan's long-held dream of a first Japan Cup win was achieved not by the two Triple Crown stars, but by supporting actor Katsuragi Ace. With this shocking win he earned the title "Triple Crown killer."\n\n* The "Hall of Fame" Horses Who Built Japanese Racing\nThis year the Hall of Fame was created to honor horses who contributed greatly to JRA racing. The first 10 were Kumohata, St. Lite, Kurifuji, Tokitsukaze, Tosa Midori, Tokino Minoru, Hakuchikara, Shinzan, Haiseiko and Tosho Boy. Mr. C.B. was inducted in 1986 and Symboli Rudolf in 1987. The "Hall of Fame Corner" in the JRA Racing Museum at Tokyo Racecourse displays bronze statues and more, telling the story of the great horses who built Japanese racing.
104=* Beyond Shinzan\nThe year before, Symboli Rudolf became unbeaten Triple Crown winner, and though beaten in the Japan Cup, won the Arima Kinen. Now he set out on the older horses' main path, aiming to be the first five-crown winner since Shinzan in 1965.\nIn the Tenno Sho (Spring), the third clash of Triple Crown winners with Mr. C.B., Rudolf let Mr. C.B. go ahead for the first time, then easily ran him down as he tired. Jockey Yukio Okabe raised five fingers - five crowns.\nNo rival seemed left, but he was injured before the Takarazuka Kinen. On his return in the Tenno Sho (Autumn) he was beaten by outsider Gallop Dyna (he is said to have shed tears of frustration in his stall afterwards). Still, trainer Yuji Nohira declared, "They say there are no certainties in racing, but with Rudolf there are," showing total faith in him. As if in reply, Rudolf shrugged off bad weather to win the Japan Cup, a sixth crown, beyond Shinzan.\nWith a trip abroad planned for the next year, the Arima Kinen was set as his last race in Japan. Nohira asked Okabe not just to win but to "win impressively." As told, Rudolf calmly took the lead at the third turn and won by 4 lengths from that year's double classic winner Miho Shinzan. He was the first to win the Arima Kinen twice since Speed Symboli in 1970 - and Japan's first-ever seven-crown winner.\n\n* Nihon Pillow Winner Dominates the Mile\nWhile the older horses' main path was thrilled by Triple Crown clashes, one horse kept fighting over a mile: Nihon Pillow Winner. In 1982 he won his first 3 starts, showing his talent, but in the next year's Satsuki Sho he was last of 20, nearly 5 seconds behind winner Mr. C.B. Giving up on the classics, his camp turned to the mile division, which now had new G1s thanks to graded races. There he showed his full talent, piling up wins including the G1 Mile Championship.\nIn '85 he won two sprint G2s, the Milers C. and the Keio Hai Spring C., then won the Yasuda Kinen, which he had missed the year before with a fracture. In his farewell race, the Mile Championship, he won clearly by 3 lengths, winning it twice and bowing out in style. That year he was champion sprinter for the third straight year.\nA great miler who starred from the very first year Japan's sprint and mile program was established, he was called "the Emperor of the Mile" after Emperor Symboli Rudolf. His trainer boasted, "At a mile he wouldn't lose even to Symboli Rudolf," but they met only once, not at a mile but in the 2000m Tenno Sho (Autumn). Rudolf was 2nd and Pillow Winner 3rd, just 0.1 seconds behind. Running so well on a stage that clearly favored Rudolf shows his strength.\n\n* Racing Tilts West\nBack then Kanto horses were far stronger. In the Japanese Derby, for example, winners from 1973 to 1985 were 10 from Kanto and just 3 from Kansai.\nWestern trainers believed "Kanto horses are strong because they train on the uphill straights at Tokyo and Nakayama." In response, in 1985 a hill training course opened at Ritto Training Center.\nWithin a few years its effects became clear, shaping the "strong West, weak East" balance that followed.
