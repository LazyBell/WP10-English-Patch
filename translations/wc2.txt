29=* Piggott's Greatest Horse\nSir Ivor was bred in America and raised in Ireland, and at first his build didn't stand out. But after breaking his maiden in his second start, he won three straight to end his 2yo season. L. Piggott, who rode him in the last of those, felt his acceleration and said "it felt as if a horse had shot out from under the saddle." With 3 wins from 4 starts, he was ranked No. 2 on the ratings.\nIn 1968 Sir Ivor and Piggott took on the British classics. In the 2000 Guineas his rival was Petingo, also ridden by Piggott in his wins, unbeaten in 3 and top-rated. Some doubted Piggott's judgment in choosing Sir Ivor over Petingo. Petingo and others raced up front while Sir Ivor waited at the back. Petingo hit the lead 2 furlongs (400m) out, but Sir Ivor burst through with a lightning kick and beat him by a length and a half.\nNext came the Derby. In a strong field Sir Ivor was the runaway favorite at 1.8, the shortest price for a post-war Derby. Again he raced at the rear. Connaught tried to hold on, but Sir Ivor swooped wide with tremendous acceleration and won by a length - a British double classic winner. The local press called his finish a "thrilling surge," and Piggott called him "the best horse I've ever ridden."\nBut in his next race, the Irish Derby, the 1.33 favorite lost to Ribero, an outsider at 17.67 - ridden by none other than his own partner Piggott. It was called the greatest upset of the 20th century. In the Eclipse S. Piggott was back aboard, but he was only 3rd. Skipping the St. Leger over doubts about the distance, he went to France for the Arc, but despite a good run he finished 2nd.\nYet Sir Ivor wasn't done. Just two weeks after the Arc he won the Champion S., beating Taj Dewan, who had finished ahead of him in the Eclipse. Riding that momentum he went to his native America for the Washington DC International. A Derby winner racing in America was very rare - the first since Papyrus in 1923, 45 years earlier. Japan's Takeshiba O also ran. Heavy rain made the going truly bad, and Laurel Racecourse (now Laurel Park) was a tight track favoring front-runners - everything was against a closer like Sir Ivor. Takeshiba O led, while Sir Ivor sat a bit further forward than usual, at the back of the midfield. In the straight he made a furious charge, swept past the field and won.\nThat was Sir Ivor's last race. Piggott, who rode on for over 20 more years, later said "he and I had a special relationship, unlike any other horse."\n\n* All In on the Arc\nThe horse that beat Sir Ivor in the Arc was the Irish-bred Vaguely Noble. He had won 2 of 4 at 2 with two 2nds, but wasn't entered in the British classics, so the owners who bought him at the end of that season set the Arc as his 3yo goal. With no French classic entries either, the Arc was really the only target.\nIn 1968, after moving to France, Vaguely Noble won his first two races. He was 3rd in the Grand Prix de Saint-Cloud but won the Prix de Chantilly and headed for the Arc. In a field of stars like Sir Ivor and Ribero he was the favorite. He and Sir Ivor raced near the front. In the straight Vaguely Noble broke clear, and even Sir Ivor's kick couldn't close the gap. Vaguely Noble had won the Arc just as planned.\nMany hoped for a second Arc, but he was retired at 3, to much regret. Rated 140 lbs, one of the highest ever, he was syndicated for a record $5 million and went to stud, where his first crop included the great mare Dahlia - success in his second career too.\n\n* The First-Ever Triple Tiara\nThis year one filly swept the US fillies' classics: Dark Mirage. She was so small she was mocked as "pony-sized" at the sale and sold for the minimum, $6,000 (2.16 million yen then). She debuted at 2 and won 2 of 15 - unimpressive, but impressive given she weighed just 323 kg. Her real success came next. In 1968, at 3, she won from her second start on, took the Kentucky Oaks, and went for the Triple Tiara (New York fillies' triple crown). She won the first leg, the Acorn S., by 6 lengths, matching the course record of past Triple Crown winner Count Fleet. No one could touch her now: she took the Mother Goose S. by 10 lengths and the CCA Oaks by 12, becoming the first-ever Triple Tiara winner.
