117=* Taiki Shuttle and Seeking the Pearl Go Abroad\nIn 1995 Fujiyama Kenzan won the first graded race abroad by a Japanese horse (the G2 Hong Kong International Cup), but a top European G1 remained out of reach. Two foreign-bred horses took up the challenge: Taiki Shuttle and Seeking the Pearl.\nTaiki Shuttle dominated the sprint-mile division in '97, winning 2 G1s and champion sprinter. In '98 he kept rolling, winning the Keio Hai Spring Cup and the Yasuda Kinen, and set off on his long-planned trip to France.\nSeeking the Pearl, a filly from the same division who won the NHK Mile Cup the year before, was also headed to France. She was first aimed at the G1 Prix Jacques le Marois over a mile, but was well beaten by Taiki Shuttle in the Yasuda Kinen just before the trip. With Taiki Shuttle now set for that race, she avoided him and targeted the G1 Prix Maurice de Gheest, 1300m on the straight, a week earlier. She was 5th choice. She broke well and, instead of racing from midfield as in Japan, easily settled near the front. Past 500m out, as the others began to push, she cruised to the lead with so much in hand her jockey could wait, then held off the rest to win from the front in course-record time - Japanese racing's long-held dream of a first European G1 for a Japan-trained horse. Her trainer Hideyuki Mori said afterwards, "Taiki Shuttle, who runs next week, is even stronger."\nIn the Prix Jacques le Marois, with top rivals out and buoyed by Seeking the Pearl's win, Taiki Shuttle was expected to win and was a clear favorite. Local fans flocked to see the Japanese horse said to be stronger than her. It was his first straight-course mile. Early on he reportedly looked around curiously (at the rail), but he made his move about 300m out, and past 200m out five horses fought side by side. Near the line he got half a length clear to win the battle.\nJapan-trained horses winning European G1s two weeks running shocked racing worldwide, gave Japanese horsemen courage and laid the foundation for later G1 challenges abroad. About 40 years after Japan-trained horses first raced overseas, a new chapter opened for Japanese racing, so long rebuffed by the world's high walls.\n\n* Silence Suzuka Never Comes Home\nAfter debuting in 1997, Silence Suzuka showed flashes of talent but didn't deliver in graded races, and his form was erratic. But at year's end, teamed with Yutaka Take for the first time in the Hong Kong International Cup, he went for a big lead, and though 5th, Take saw something.\nIn '98, sensing promise in front-running, Take guided him to an easy win in the Valentine S. He led by a mile again in the G2 Nakayama Kinen and won, never letting anyone near. With his natural speed now in full flow, no one could catch him: with unheard-of runaway tactics, he won 5 graded races in a row, including the Takarazuka Kinen.\nThen came the Tenno Sho (Autumn). Foreign-breds El Condor Pasa and Grass Wonder weren't even eligible then, and many, including Air Groove, avoided him, so only 12 ran in a field of 18 spots. He was overwhelming favorite at 1.2. Everyone expected another dazzling escape.\nHe broke perfectly, led alone and was over 10 lengths clear before the third turn. Just as everyone was sure of victory, he sank back. A murmur swept the crowd. He had shattered a leg. Unable to stand, he was judged beyond saving and put down on the spot.\nIf he had run in the Japan Cup... if he had made his planned trip to America... many fans still dream of how "the shadowless runaway" might have run.\n\n* The Rise of the '98 Generation\nThe "'98 generation," 3yos in 1998, was packed with stars and called "the strongest generation" or "the golden generation." It always comes up in debates over the best crop ever.\nDouble classic winner Seiun Sky (Satsuki Sho and Kikuka Sho), Derby winner Special Week, who beat him there, El Condor Pasa, who won the Japan Cup at 3, and Arima Kinen winner Grass Wonder fought fiercely, none giving an inch. Together they won every flat G1 of the time, and many fans call them the greatest generation in Japanese history.\nThey kept up their fierce battles in the following years, thrilling racing.
118=* The Arc Challenge and the "Commander-in-Chief's" Revenge\nThe '98 generation, which ran riot the year before, kept thrilling racing as older horses. In fact '99 may have been their most brilliant year.\nEl Condor Pasa, a foreign-bred barred from the classics, went abroad. The final goal was the Arc, the world's greatest race. No one wins it fresh off the plane, so a campaign was planned backwards from it. He was 2nd in the Prix d'Ispahan, then won the Grand Prix de Saint-Cloud, his first G1 abroad - and the first French middle-to-long-distance G1 ever won by a Japan-trained horse.\nThe Prix Foy prep had just 3 runners; he won it and moved on to the Arc. Favorite was French and Irish Derby winner Montjeu, with El Condor Pasa 2nd choice, already highly rated by locals. Using his inside draw, he took the lead. Leading the field into the straight with plenty in hand, he gradually extended it to about 2 lengths and seemed safe, but from about 400m out Montjeu closed relentlessly from behind. About 100m out Montjeu went by; El Condor Pasa fought back but fell half a length short. The Arc dream was over, but local fans warmly applauded the Japanese horse's brave run.\nEl Condor Pasa then retired, and Montjeu's next race was, of all things, the Japan Cup. Waiting for him was Special Week, winner of both Tenno Sho races that year. Arc winner and Europe's best Montjeu was favorite, with Special Week only 2nd choice, but Special Week, racing at the back, produced the fastest finish to win, keeping Montjeu and the rest at bay. As "Japan's commander-in-chief," he avenged his great rival.\n\n* Grass vs Special: The Last Epic of the 20th Century\nGrass Wonder won his first races easily after debuting in 1997, took the Asahi Hai Sansai S. in record time, and was seen as the best of his crop. But a fracture kept him out the next spring, and after returning he lacked sparkle, until in the year-end Arima Kinen he beat double classic winner Seiun Sky, Tenno Sho winner Mejiro Bright and empress Air Groove to make a full comeback.\nIn '99 he met Derby winner Special Week for the first time in the Takarazuka Kinen. One was rated the best of the crop at his debut; the other had become it by winning the Derby and Tenno Sho (Spring). Their clash drew huge attention, and the betting split between them, well ahead of the rest - a two-horse showdown. Special Week sat just ahead of midfield, with Grass Wonder right behind, marking him. Past 600m out Special Week took the lead, with Grass Wonder chasing. A match race loomed at the final turn, but Grass Wonder easily passed Special Week and won by 3 lengths, as if saying he had never given up the title of best of the crop.\nThey clashed again in the Arima Kinen. Grass Wonder had skipped the Japan Cup after a setback following his Mainichi Okan win, while Special Week came in fully prepared after winning the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and the Japan Cup, seeking revenge. Grass Wonder was 2.8 and Special Week 3.0. The new wide bet on the pair was hugely popular at 4.7. The pace was very slow, with Grass Wonder near the back and Special Week last. The race came alive after the third turn: Grass Wonder swept forward wide with Special Week chasing, and the battle went into the straight. Tsurumaru Tsuyoshi briefly led, T.M. Opera O, the Satsuki Sho winner, came up the inside, and Grass Wonder and Special Week flew wide; four horses hit the line almost together, in the same time. After a long photo, Grass Wonder won by just 4 cm. After enduring an era unkind to foreign-breds, he had won three straight Grand Prix races.\n\n* The Hero Comes From the North\nMeisei Opera won 9 straight at places like Iwate, beat JRA horses in the '98 G1 Nambu Hai, and was hailed as the second coming of Iwate's "monster" Tokei Nisei. This year he chose the JRA's G1 February S. for his first race. Some called a local horse taking on a JRA G1 reckless, but local fans believed "Meisei Opera can do it." Against JRA dirt stars like Washington Color and M.I. Blanc, he was a confident 2nd choice. He handled the turf start easily, sat in a good spot, surged powerfully down the middle of the track in the straight, took the lead easily past 200m out and won by 2 lengths. He was the first local-based horse ever to win a JRA G1, and none has done it since.
119=* "End-of-Century King": An Era of One\nThe year before, T.M. Opera O fought a fierce Big Three battle in the classics with Narita Top Road and Admire Vega and was named champion 3yo colt. In 2000, with "strongest generation" stars like Special Week and El Condor Pasa gone, he ran riot among the older horses.\nHe won the G2 Kyoto Kinen and Hanshin Daishoten, then went to the Tenno Sho (Spring). Foreign-breds could run from this year, so a clash with Grass Wonder of the "strongest generation" was hoped for. But Grass Wonder hadn't been right since the last Arima Kinen and skipped it to focus on the Takarazuka Kinen. Opera O beat Rascal Suzuka and others easily for his second G1, his first since the Satsuki Sho a year before.\nIn the Takarazuka Kinen he finally met Grass Wonder. Opera O was 1.9 and Grass Wonder 2.8 - a two-horse fight. But Opera O came wide at the final turn and ground down the field with a dogged finish to win. Grass Wonder couldn't quicken and sank to 6th. Having won the older horses' spring G1s, he was now the best horse in training.\nHe kept rolling, winning the Tenno Sho (Autumn) safely. In the Japan Cup he drew a record 50.5% of win bets, and lived up to it, winning a fierce duel with Meisho Doto by a neck. His career earnings passed 1.2 billion yen, beating Special Week's record to become world No. 1.\nHe went into the Arima Kinen unbeaten in 7 that year, topping the fan vote with over 100,000 votes. He broke well, but several horses marked him, his path was blocked, and he dropped to 10th or worse into the straight. Boxed in and late to move, he seemed beaten; the crowd groaned. But in mid-straight he threaded through the pack, passed about 10 horses and snatched victory. Meisho Doto was 2nd again - beaten by Opera O in all four G1s that year. Their many great duels are still talked about.\nWith the Arima Kinen, Opera O became the first to sweep the older horses' middle-to-long G1s. He also set records like a record 5 G1s in a year and the first 8 straight graded wins as favorite, and was unanimously Horse of the Year - truly the "end-of-century king" who ruled the turf.\n\n* Beyond the Border Between Japan and the World\nThis year Hong Kong's Fairy King Prawn won the Yasuda Kinen, the first Japanese G1 won by a Hong Kong horse. Meanwhile Junko Hosoe, one of the "brilliant Class 12," became the first JRA female jockey to win abroad. Foreign horses racing in Japan, and Japanese horses and jockeys going abroad, grew year after year.
120=* Agnes Tachyon, Who Ran at the Speed of Light\nAgnes Tachyon, full brother to the previous year's Derby winner Agnes Flight, was watched from the start. His workouts weren't eye-catching, so he was only 3rd choice on debut, but he won by three and a half lengths. In the Radio Tampa Hai Sansai S. (now the Hopeful S.), against fellow classic hopes Jungle Pocket and Kurofune, he won in record time and became the classic favorite.\nHis first race of 2001 was the Yayoi Sho. When he entered, others pulled out one after another - his strength was already recognized. The going was bad, seen as a disadvantage for a horse who won with a finishing kick. But he won easily by 5 lengths, with future Kikuka Sho winner Manhattan Cafe 4th.\nThen the first classic, the Satsuki Sho. With stablemate Agnes Gold, another classic hope, out injured, the betting piled onto Tachyon, who took 59.6% of win bets - the second-highest in Satsuki Sho history after Tokino Minoru (1951). Sitting in a good spot, he took the lead easily in the straight. Dantsu Flame and Jungle Pocket closed hard, but near the line he pulled away again with plenty left.\nWith hopes high for brothers winning the Derby, bad news came in May: he had a tendon injury. He was retired that summer, leaving the turf after just 4 races. That Kurofune later won the NHK Mile C. and the Japan Cup Dirt, and Jungle Pocket the Derby and the Japan Cup - both 2-time G1 winners - shows how strong Tachyon was. At stud he passed that strength on, siring many G1 winners like Daiwa Scarlet and Deep Sky.\n\n* The Relentless Challenger, Meisho Doto\nThe year before, T.M. Opera O won 5 G1s (8 straight graded races) among older horses. Four of those times, Meisho Doto was 2nd. The public called him "the silver collector who can never beat Opera O." But only in the Tenno Sho (Autumn) was he beaten clearly; the rest were by a neck twice and a nose once - defeats that still showed his strength.\nAt 5 this year, Meisho Doto didn't give up. He met Opera O for the 5th time in the Tenno Sho (Spring). Having always lost by racing ahead and being passed in the straight, he tried sitting behind Opera O for the first time. But despite a desperate drive in the straight, he fell short and was 2nd again.\nHis next try was the Takarazuka Kinen, where a year before, in his first G1, he had first lost to Opera O. As Opera O was hampered and couldn't get clear, Meisho Doto raced forward and led at the final turn. The gap closed in the straight, but he held on, finally beating Opera O at their 6th meeting. It was also his first G1 title.\n\n* Japanese Racing Goes International\nThis year Stay Gold won the Dubai Sheema Classic, the first graded win in Dubai by a Japan-trained horse. As this shows, Japanese racing was catching up with the world year by year, and international trading of racehorses had grown since the 1990s.\nJapan had counted horses' ages by the traditional system, counting a horse as 1 at birth. But the world used actual age, causing a one-year gap (a horse aged 2 worldwide was 3 in Japan). To avoid confusion in international trade, from 2001 Japan switched to the current actual-age system.\nRacing itself also opened up. This year the Japanese Derby and the Kikuka Sho were opened to foreign-bred horses, and the other classics followed in later years as Japanese racing rapidly internationalized.
121=* A 3yo's Challenge\nThe foreign-bred wave that began in the 1990s kept going into the 2000s. Foreign-breds like Eagle Cafe (2000 NHK Mile C.), Agnes Digital (2001 Tenno Sho (Autumn)) and Kurofune (2001 Japan Cup Dirt) were still thrilling racing. The best of them in the 2000s was Symboli Kris S.\nHe won his debut the year before, but a fragile constitution kept him from winning again, and he couldn't get into the Satsuki Sho. So he aimed for the Derby via a trial, winning his first graded race, the Aoba Sho (Derby trial), and went into the Japanese Derby 3rd choice behind 3-time graded winner Tanino Gimlet and Satsuki Sho winner No Reason. The Derby had been opened to foreign-breds the year before, when Kurofune was 5th. Hopes were high that he'd be the first foreign-bred Derby winner, but Tanino Gimlet swooped just before the line and he was 2nd. By fate, Gimlet was ridden by Yutaka Take, who had guided Kris S. to victory in the Aoba Sho.\nHis true worth showed in autumn. After a smooth summer he won the Kobe Shimbun Hai, then skipped the Kikuka Sho, the last classic, for the Tenno Sho (Autumn) against older horses. He was good enough to win the 3000m Kikuka Sho, but his camp felt he had a better chance in the 2000m Tenno Sho, even against older horses.\nThe 2002 Tenno Sho (Autumn) was held at Nakayama for the first time in 35 years, since 1967, due to renovation at Tokyo. The field held seasoned older horses like Narita Top Road, 3 graded wins that year and 3rd in the Tenno Sho (Spring), and T.M. Ocean, the previous year's fillies' double winner, fresh from winning the Sapporo Kinen. Kris S. was the only 3yo. He broke well and raced on the inside just ahead of midfield. Hemmed in by the leaders at the final turn, he forced his way through a tiny gap in the straight and burst clear with a powerful finish, beating Narita Top Road, who came wide, by three-quarters of a length for his first G1. Only the third 3yo ever to win the Tenno Sho (Autumn).\nIn the Japan Cup a slow start cost him, and he couldn't catch two lightly-regarded foreign horses who broke clear first, finishing 3rd. Perhaps because of that loss, he was 2nd choice for the Arima Kinen behind 3yo filly Fine Motion, unbeaten in 6 with wins in the Shuka Sho and Queen Elizabeth II Cup - two 3yos as top two choices, a strange situation. Tap Dance City raised the pace down the backstretch to open a gap, and as the others moved early to close it, Kris S. waited until the straight, then charged and caught Tap Dance City alone to win. He became the first horse to win the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and the Arima Kinen at 3, and was named Horse of the Year.\n\n* Sunday Silence Dies\nSince becoming leading sire in 1995, when his first crop swept the classics (Satsuki Sho: Genuine, Derby: Tayasu Tsuyoshi, Oaks: Dance Partner), Sunday Silence reigned on, redrawing Japan's bloodline map. But this year he developed laminitis and died, much mourned. His offspring kept winning after his death, and he stayed leading sire for 13 years until 2007. His blood lives on in his descendants and has recently begun spreading worldwide - truly a great sire Japan can be proud of.\n\n* IPAT Begins\nBy now mobile phones with web browsers were spreading fast. The JRA began running "IPAT," internet betting for mobile phones. Betting from home already existed, but now buying tickets became even easier, which helped racing spread further.
122=* Still in Love: The Second Fillies' Triple Crown\nThis year's fillies' classics were split between two daughters of Sunday Silence. Favorite in all three legs was Admire Groove, a blueblood out of "noble empress" and Horse of the Year Air Groove; she won 3 straight against colts before the Oka Sho. 2nd choice in all three was Still in Love. With a graded-winning brother (Big Bayamon, winner of the Radio Tampa Sho), she was watched early, won her first two, and was a gallant 2nd despite trouble in the Tulip Sho trial to earn her Oka Sho spot.\nIn the Oka Sho, Admire Groove shockingly missed the break and had to race from behind. Still in Love raced in a good spot, burst powerfully out of the middle of the track in the straight and won by a length and a half. Admire Groove closed wide but could only finish 3rd - a clear win for Still in Love.\nThe Oaks held special meaning for Admire Groove: a chance for three generations of winners after her granddam Dyna Carl and dam Air Groove. Three generations or a fillies' double? Again Admire Groove missed the break - in her 3yo spring this habit was her great trial. She was nearly last at the final turn, and the gap to Still in Love, who surged sharply from midfield to win, only grew. A double won on ability over popularity - it was clear to all that the true heroine was Still in Love.\nBoth camps chose the Rose S. for their autumn start. Still in Love was favorite for the first time, but Admire Groove won and she was 5th. The heroine's crown seemed to pass to Admire Groove. Then came the last leg, the Shuka Sho. Still in Love stuck to her own style, going forward, breaking clear and holding on, holding off Admire Groove by three-quarters of a length. Showing unmatched big-race grit, she became only the second fillies' Triple Crown winner, after Mejiro Ramonu in 1986. The Shuka Sho was created in 1996 as the third leg when the Queen Elizabeth II Cup opened to older mares; this was the first Triple Crown since.\nAmong colts, Neo Universe won the Satsuki Sho and the Japanese Derby but missed the Triple Crown, so no colt-and-filly double Triple Crown. Still, double classic winners among both colts and fillies in the same year was a first in 28 years, since 1975 (Kabuto Yao and Tesco Gaby).\n\n* Hishi Miracle's Two Long-Shot Wins\nIn the previous Kikuka Sho, with favorite No Reason losing his rider at the start, Hishi Miracle swept forward from around the third turn and held on to win as 10th choice - a "miracle" true to his name. But he was 11th in the Arima Kinen, 12th in the Hanshin Daishoten and 7th in the Osaka Hai this year, and was being branded a "fluke Kikuka Sho winner."\nThen came the Tenno Sho (Spring). Despite a weak field with only one other G1 winner, Dantsu Flame, he was only 7th choice. But he defied the odds, holding off Sunrise Jaeger's charge by half a length.\nThe Takarazuka Kinen drew a stellar field, including last year's Horse of the Year Symboli Kris S. and Agnes Digital, a G1 winner on turf and dirt. The day before, one person's huge bet made Hishi Miracle the 1.7 favorite - a strange event (the bet did win) - though he settled at 6th choice (16.3). Racing in midfield, he rounded the final turn 8th, then charged to the front in the straight. Turu Maru Boy came flying wide and drew level at the end, but he held on by a neck. As if to say "don't call it a fluke anymore," he won his third G1, proving his ability.\n\n* Symboli Kris S. Wins the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and Arima Kinen Again\nThe year before, Symboli Kris S. won the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and Arima Kinen at 3 and was Horse of the Year. Big things were expected, but in his first race, the Takarazuka Kinen without a prep, he ran into Hishi Miracle's long-shot win and was a surprise 5th. But again he came good in autumn. In the Tenno Sho (Autumn), shrugging off the widest draw and the fast pace set by Lohengrin and Go Steady fighting for the lead, he burst clear a third of the way out from the rail in the straight to become the first to win the Tenno Sho (Autumn) twice (Mejiro McQueen and T.M. Opera O had won the spring race twice).\nIn the Japan Cup he let Tap Dance City get away alone and was well beaten in 3rd, over 9 lengths back. Then his farewell, the Arima Kinen. Racing in midfield, he moved up from before the third turn, drew level with the leaders easily at the final turn, and simply drew away, winning by 9 lengths - the biggest margin in a G1 since graded races began in 1984.\nThe first to win the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and Arima Kinen twice each, he was Horse of the Year for the second straight year, matching his great namesake Symboli Rudolf (1984 and 1985), and left the turf in glory.
