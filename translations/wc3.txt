30=* The Prince and the Rival Who Stood in His Way\nIn September 1967, at the Keeneland Sale in America, a yearling sold for $250,000, then a record: Majestic Prince. His commanding build, rare for a yearling, won buyers over, and he debuted to win both his starts at 2.\nIn 1969, at 3, Majestic Prince stretched his streak to 7 and went into the Kentucky Derby, the first leg of the Triple Crown, unbeaten. The field held stars like Top Knight, the previous year's champion 2yo colt, and Arts and Letters, who had won a prep by 15 lengths, so many rivals pulled out and only 8 ran. In the straight Majestic Prince and Arts and Letters fought it out, and the Prince held on by a neck. In the Preakness S. the two dueled again, and again he won, this time by a head. No horse had ever won both races unbeaten, and racing fans went wild. By now he was affectionately known as "The Prince."\nJust as everyone expected him to go for an unbeaten Triple Crown, his connections held a press conference the morning after the Preakness and announced he would skip the Belmont S. America hadn't seen a Triple Crown winner in years, and Forward Pass had fallen just short the year before, so hopes were high; a sports magazine wrote that "the Prince ran away from the big one." Under the pressure of public opinion, his camp reversed course and entered the Belmont.\nThe third leg, the Belmont S. Majestic Prince was rumored to have leg problems, while Arts and Letters was in top form after beating older horses in the Metropolitan H. The betting was tight: the Prince favorite at 2.3, Arts and Letters second choice at 2.7. Arts and Letters went forward at the start and stayed ahead of the Prince throughout. In the straight Majestic Prince chased desperately, but Arts and Letters pulled away to win by five and a half lengths. The dream of an unbeaten Triple Crown was ended by the rival he had battled all along.\nMajestic Prince then went on a long break and retired without racing again. With "The Prince" gone, Arts and Letters led American racing, winning 6 straight and taking champion 3yo colt.\n\n* A Bargain Mare: Park Top\nBorn in Britain, the filly Park Top was bought cheaply and, due to health problems, couldn't debut at 2 - an ordinary horse. But when she debuted at 3 she won four straight. She kept running well, and in 1969, at 5, she took off.\nThat year Park Top had won the Prix de la Seine, the Coronation C. and the Hardwicke S. With L. Piggott aboard she took on the big stage of the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S. and won in fine style. Aiming for the Arc, she also won the prep, the Prix Foy. Winning the King George and the Arc in the same year would be a first for a mare, and on Arc day she was 2nd favorite. But it was 14th choice Levmoss who broke clear. Overlooked because he had been racing over long distances, he held on with a long run as if to show off a stayer's stamina. He never tired, and as the others fell away only Park Top came at him wide - but perhaps because she was slow to get out of the pack, she couldn't catch him, losing by three-quarters of a length. She just missed history, but she was a mare who rose from bargain buy to brilliant runner.
31=* Nijinsky, Dancing on the Turf\nNijinsky was a Canadian-bred, Irish-trained horse named after the legendary Russian ballet dancer Vaslav Nijinsky, who late in life reportedly said "if I am reborn, I want to be a horse." True to his name, Nijinsky ran with elegance on the turf. He won all 5 starts at 2 and was named champion 2yo colt in both Britain and Ireland.\nIn 1970 he opened in the Gladness S. against older horses. The bad going didn't suit a closer like him, but he won by 5 lengths without being asked. In the 2000 Guineas, the first classic, he was the overwhelming favorite at 1.57. Sitting at the back of midfield, he took off 3 furlongs (600m) out and won by two and a half lengths, so easily that jockey L. Piggott looked back over his shoulder. Some said the Derby's 1 mile 4 furlongs (2400m) was too far for him, and his odds drifted to about 2.4, the longest of his career. He also had colic two days before and went in barely eating. But he burst out of midfield to win comfortably by two and a half lengths, taking his second classic. Nijinsky's camp had invited Vaslav's widow Romola to the race; she was delighted with the horse and is said to have bet heavily on him every time he ran.\nHe kept rolling, winning the Irish Derby by three and a half lengths without being asked, then beat older horses easily in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S. as the only 3yo. But over the summer he caught ringworm (a fungal skin infection). At one point he lost all the hair on one flank and couldn't even be saddled. Thanks to devoted care he recovered and was ready to race. The Arc was his main goal, but Doncaster, home of the St. Leger S. - the third leg of the British Triple Crown - pleaded for him to run, so he did. At about 15 furlongs (about 3000m) it was further than he had ever gone, but hopes for the first Triple Crown winner in 35 years made him a heavy favorite. He raced at the back, made his move in the straight, took the lead easily and won by a length - the 15th British Triple Crown winner, and only the 4th unbeaten.\nIn Europe today, winning at each horse's best distance counts for more than versatility, so few horses aim for the Triple Crown, and none has won it since Nijinsky. But from 2025 Betfred, the big bookmaker sponsoring the 2000 Guineas, offers a GBP 2 million (about 410 million yen) bonus to a Triple Crown winner, so the trend may change.\n\n* The Arc's Huge Upset\nThe favorite for that year's Arc was Nijinsky, who came to France to sweep Europe's three big races (called the "European Triple Crown" in Japan). But the St. Leger had taken a heavy toll: he had lost about 30 lbs (about 13.5 kg). Far from his best, his popularity no longer depended on form, and he was favorite at 1.4. The leading runners went forward while Nijinsky sat at the back. In the straight 4th choice Sassafras went to the front. Nijinsky chased and finally passed him with about 100m left. But the moment Piggott hit him right-handed to pull away, Nijinsky veered sharply left, and Sassafras seized the chance to fight back. After a photo finish Sassafras won by a head. Longchamp reportedly fell silent at the shock. It was the first defeat of the great Nijinsky's career.\nSassafras retired after the Arc, while Nijinsky tried to bounce back in the Champion S. But he sweated badly before the race and was beaten into 2nd again. That was his last race.
