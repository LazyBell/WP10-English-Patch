48=* Reference Point's British Double\nMill Reef was the first to win the European Triple Crown (as Japan calls it), and his son Reference Point aimed for it too. In his first season he won the William Hill Futurity S., the 2yo mile championship, for his first G1.\nIn 1987 he was aimed at the 2000 Guineas, the first classic, but a nasal infection forced him out. On his return he won a G2 easily and went for the Derby, the second classic. He led from the start, and when a rival drew level in the straight he found a second wind and pulled away to win by a length and a half, joining his sire as a Derby winner.\nHe lost to older horses in the Eclipse S., but was favorite for the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. Again he made the running, never gave up the lead and won by 3 lengths, one step from the European Triple Crown.\nHis camp then planned the St. Leger before the Arc. In the past 50 years only 3 Derby winners had run in the St. Leger - an unorthodox plan. But fans were excited, and he was a heavy favorite at 1.36. Again he led all the way to the line, completing a British double.\nThen, for his first trip abroad, the Arc. Expected to be the first European Triple Crown winner since his sire Mill Reef, he was again a clear favorite at 1.73. As usual he went to the front, but Sharaniya took him on. Their duel made the pace furious. Reference Point just held the lead into the straight, but had no stamina left to hold off the field, sank back into the pack and was a well-beaten 8th. Winner Trempolino broke the Arc record set the year before by Dancing Brave by 1.4 seconds.\nFounded as an unusual race for 3yos only in an era when 5- and 6-year-olds dominated, the St. Leger had long been prestigious. But after Triple Crown winner Nijinsky lost the Arc two weeks after the St. Leger in 1970, its prestige fell, and Reference Point's heavy Arc defeat is said to have finished it off. His loss may have had other causes, such as the blistering pace or an abscess in his foot, but in fact no Derby winner ran in the St. Leger for the next 25 years.
49=* A Legendary Breeders' Cup Distaff\nThis year's Breeders' Cup Distaff brought together the three best fillies and mares of the time in a fierce battle still remembered as a race of the century.\nOne was Personal Ensign, unbeaten in 12 starts - a seasoned 4yo with 7 G1 wins. Another was Winning Colors, only the third filly to win the Kentucky Derby. The third was Goodbye Halo, who had won the fillies' G1 series: the Kentucky Oaks, Mother Goose S. and CCA Oaks. To see the three clash, crowds packed Churchill Downs despite bad weather.\nWinning Colors went to the front and tried to make the sloppy track work for her, but Personal Ensign came with a stunning finish to win by a nose. Goodbye Halo, who closed with her, was 3rd by half a length. Personal Ensign retired after this race, the first horse in 80 years to retire unbeaten after 10 or more starts.\nAt the Eclipse Awards, Horse of the Year went to Breeders' Cup Classic winner Alysheba, but Personal Ensign was named champion older mare and Winning Colors champion 3yo filly.\nThat year's Breeders' Cup also saw the great European mare Miesque win the Breeders' Cup Mile for the second time, a first. It was her only race in America that year, but her dominant performance earned her the Eclipse Award as champion turf mare. That day brought together the great mares of America and Europe - a historic day.\n\n* Italy's Great Tony Bin Wins the Arc\nIn Europe the big story was Tony Bin, the first Italian-trained Arc winner in 27 years. Italy had produced greats like Nearco and Ribot, but its racing declined from the 1970s and fell behind Britain, Ireland and France. No Italian horse had won the Arc since Molvedo in 1961. Tony Bin appeared as if to blow away that gloom.\nTony Bin was no star prospect at first; he was a late bloomer who only came into his own at 4. The year before he was 5th in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. and 2nd in the Arc, showing world-class ability, and this year he became the first Italian-trained Arc winner in 26 years, bringing joy to his homeland.\nMtoto, beaten into 2nd by Tony Bin, was Britain's undisputed ace, having won the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. and the Eclipse S. Losing to a horse from Italy, seen as a lesser racing nation, left Britain in deep dismay.\nSoon after, Tony Bin announced he would run in the Japan Cup. The first Arc winner ever to run in the race was big news in Japan. Sadly he couldn't keep up with the epic battle between Pay the Butler and Tamamo Cross and finished 5th, just behind the fight for 3rd.
50=* After Four Battles...\nIn the year Secretariat, the legend hailed as America's greatest, passed away, two new heroes appeared who would carve their names into US racing history: Sunday Silence and Easy Goer, both familiar to Japanese fans.\nIn American racing at the time, Sunday Silence was just an "ordinary horse" with nothing special in his birth or pedigree, who won over doubters by winning races. Easy Goer, by contrast, came from a great farm with a first-class pedigree - an elite path promised before he was born. Sunday Silence's home, Stone Farm, was even an offshoot of Claiborne Farm, where Easy Goer was born, which says something about their relationship.\nThe two clashed in the classics, and Sunday Silence won both the Kentucky Derby and the Preakness S., with Easy Goer 2nd behind him. But in the Belmont S., the third leg, Easy Goer vented his frustration with a powerful run, crushing Sunday Silence by 8 lengths to take the last classic and show his pride. The most talked-about of their clashes was the Preakness: the fiery match race of the two, who broke clear early, was called "the race of the half-century."\nHaving split the Triple Crown, they clashed again in the Breeders' Cup Classic, and Sunday Silence held off Easy Goer by a neck to win the race of the century. As a result, Sunday Silence was named Horse of the Year and champion 3yo colt by a near-unanimous vote. Easy Goer had a flawless record too, 6 G1 wins and three 2nds, but got almost no votes and won no titles.\nUS racing's new stars weren't only horses but a jockey too: K. Desormeaux, in his 4th year, won an astonishing 598 races that year alone and was named Eclipse champion jockey. The record of 598 wins in a year still stands and remains a Guinness World Record.\n\n* Nashwan, Acclaimed in Britain\nMeanwhile in Britain, one horse drew attention rivaling legends like Nijinsky and Mill Reef: Nashwan. Unbeaten since his debut, he won the 2000 Guineas and the Derby, brushed aside older horses by 5 lengths in the Eclipse S., and went on to win the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. - a remarkable feat. Rather than the St. Leger and the Triple Crown, he chose the Arc, but in the Prix Niel prep he was a shocking 3rd. That was his last race. Nashwan, once hoped to be the first British Triple Crown winner in 19 years, divided opinion after the baffling loss and sudden retirement, and was rated a middling 131 lbs in the International Classification.\nTwo of his crop were rated higher: Old Vic and Zilzal. Old Vic won the French and Irish Derbies; he skipped the Derby for the French Derby, so he never met Nashwan. Zilzal was a dominant miler who debuted in May and retired in November, appearing and vanishing like a gust of wind.\nNashwan's camp objected to being rated below these two, and trainer D. Hern praised him above all: "the best horse I have ever trained."\n\n* A Southern Hemisphere Mare Makes Her Name Worldwide\nAlso big news that year: a New Zealand mare set a world record for 2400m on turf - Horlicks, winner of the 9th Japan Cup.\nUntil the year before she had raced only in New Zealand, and had only just won her first G1 in neighboring Australia that year. She then traveled to Japan for the Japan Cup and, after an epic battle with Oguri Cap, won in a world-record 2:22.2. The first Southern Hemisphere-trained winner of the Japan Cup, she made her name known worldwide with that astonishing time. Japan was in the middle of its second racing boom, and because she beat Oguri Cap, the idol horse of the era, the name "Horlicks" became known even to people who knew nothing about racing.
51=* Salsabil's British Fillies' Double\nThe British-trained filly Salsabil was from the second crop of Sadler's Wells, a sire well known in Japan, and one of the offspring who made him a great sire. She debuted in 1989 and won her first G1, the Prix Marcel Boussac, in her 3rd start as 4th choice.\nIn 1990 she started in the G3 Fred Darling S. Dead Certain and Chimes of Freedom, seen as leading classic fillies, were in the field, and Salsabil was 3rd choice behind them. Dead Certain led, but faded badly late, and Salsabil swept past to lead, then pulled away to win by 6 lengths.\nNow the top classic prospect, she was favorite for the 1000 Guineas. She won a fierce duel with 2nd choice Heart of Joy by three-quarters of a length. Naturally she was favorite again in the Oaks. She was blocked at one point, but once clear she pulled away to win by 5 lengths, her second classic.\nAfter resting until autumn, she skipped the St. Leger and the fillies' Triple Crown and aimed for the Arc. She won the Prix Vermeille prep by a neck and was favorite in the Arc. But she couldn't show her true form and was well beaten in 10th. She retired after this race.\nSalsabil left the turf to much regret, but her 4 G1 wins helped make her sire Sadler's Wells leading sire for the first time.\n\n* Great Sire Northern Dancer Dies\nThis year the great Canadian-born sire Northern Dancer died. He produced many top horses, including British Triple Crown winner Nijinsky from his second crop, and was leading sire twice in America and four times in Britain.\nHe was retired from stud in 1987 due to falling fertility and died in 1990, much mourned. In his place his son Sadler's Wells rose as a sire. In Japan his son Northern Taste was leading sire 10 times, and his blood has spread around the world.
