52=* "Precocious Genius" Arazi\nIn 1991 the Cartier Awards were founded as Europe's annual honors, modeled on America's Eclipse Awards. The very first Horse of the Year was, remarkably, a 2yo: Arazi.\nArazi was American-bred and French-trained. He debuted early, in May at 2, finishing 2nd. After winning a listed race he took the G3 Prix du Bois and the G2 Prix Robert Papin in a row. With an overwhelming kick that swept past the field in the straight, he went on to win the Prix Morny, the Prix de la Salamandre and the Grand Criterium.\nHaving swept France's 2yo G1s, he went to his native America for the Breeders' Cup Juvenile, the 2yo colts' championship. His first race on dirt and a wide draw raised doubts, but he was still favorite at 3.1. He sat at the back early, hit the front around the final turn, and kept pulling away to win by 5 lengths - the first European-trained winner of the race.\nWith 7 wins from 8 that year, including 4 G1s, Arazi was named the first-ever Cartier Horse of the Year.\n\n* Generous: From Huge Upset to Top Class\nThe Irish-bred colt Generous was, true to his name, said to be a gentle horse. He debuted early, in May at 2. He won first time out, but in his first G1, the Prix Morny, he was well beaten in 10th, and in his next G1 try, the Dewhurst S., he was 7th choice at 51. Held up at the back, he came with a tremendous kick to win by three-quarters of a length - a huge upset.\nIn 1991 he opened his 3yo year with a respectable 4th in the 2000 Guineas, enough to show his win wasn't a fluke - and from there his run began. In the Derby, perhaps because it was his first try at 12 furlongs (2400m), he drifted to 5th choice. Tracking in 3rd, he broke clear about 2 furlongs out and pulled away to win by 5 lengths, a rare rout even for the Derby.\nNow in the top class, he won the Irish Derby as favorite, then crushed older horses by a race-record 7 lengths in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. He seemed off form in the Arc and was a well-beaten 8th, and illness afterwards forced his retirement. At the new Cartier Awards, Horse of the Year went to Arazi and champion 3yo colt to Suave Dancer, but in the International Classification he was rated 137 lbs, above both.\nAt stud he stood 4 years in Britain before moving to Japan, where he sired Erimo Harrier, three-time winner of the Hakodate Kinen.
53=* User Friendly's British Fillies' Double\nUser Friendly was a filly bred in Britain by W. Gredley. By Slip Anchor, part of three generations of Derby winners, out of a mare by Derby winner Blakeney, her pedigree was excellent - yet she found no buyer as a yearling, so Gredley kept her.\nShe finally debuted in April 1992 at 3, an outsider at 26, and won by two and a half lengths. Two weeks later she won the Oaks Trial S. and suddenly became a classic contender. In the Oaks she was 3rd choice at 6. She raced near the front and took the lead early. By the time the others began to close it was too late: she won by three and a half lengths, with the 3rd another 20 lengths back. Beating the favorite in dominant style, she received great applause. She went on to win the Irish Oaks and Yorkshire Oaks, three Oaks in all (in Japan the winner of all three is sometimes called an Anglo-Irish Oaks Triple Crown winner).\nNext was the St. Leger. She was the only filly among 7 runners, yet favorite ahead of the colts. Again she raced forward, led about 2 furlongs (400m) out, and won by three and a half lengths, completing the British fillies' double. She also finished 2nd in an Arc packed with top older colts, and with such a brilliant record was named Cartier Horse of the Year.\n\n* Nijinsky's Legacy\nAfter racing, British Triple Crown winner Nijinsky succeeded Northern Dancer as a top sire, and in 1986, thanks to his son Shahrastani, he was champion sire in Britain and Ireland. Through horses like Maruzensky his influence on Japan was also great.\nIn 1992, as his illness worsened, Nijinsky was put down. As the great horse departed, his son Lammtarra, born that year, would go on to achieve what even his sire could not.
54=* First European-Trained Breeders' Cup Classic Winner\nSagace missed a second Arc by a whisker after a bitter disqualification. He went to stud in America but died young at 9, leaving only three crops. One of his few offspring was Arcangues. Trained in France, Arcangues began with a dismal last of 19 on debut, and by 4 had a plain record of 3 wins from 11, including a G2 and a G3.\nAt 5 he started in the Prix Ganay and was 7th of 8. Still he kept running in G1s, choosing the Prix d'Ispahan next. After his poor run he was only 5th choice of 7. Usually a closer, this time he raced forward, never weakened in the straight, and won his first G1 by a length and a half. Encouraged, he traveled to England to avenge his loss in the Eclipse S. the year before, but was a dismal 6th. Back home in the G2 Prix Dollar he was a distant 4th, 10 lengths back.\nAfter all those losses, his camp chose the Breeders' Cup Classic, which gathers the world's best dirt horses. After a heavy defeat, from a bad wide draw and, above all, in his first dirt race, he was given no chance: least fancied at 134.6. His regular jockey was unavailable, so J. Bailey rode at short notice. Unable to speak French, he couldn't understand the trainers, and reportedly said, "I know nothing about this horse - I don't even know how to pronounce his name." With everything pointing to defeat, the race began. Favorite Bertrando took the lead and set a fast pace into the straight. No one seemed able to catch him, but Arcangues came flying wide with a superb finish, swept past and beat Bertrando by 2 lengths. The least-fancied horse became the first European-trained winner of the Breeders' Cup Classic. The commentator called it: "Absolutely shocking! Impossible victory!" His 134.6 odds are still the biggest payout in Breeders' Cup history.
55=* "The Conquering King of Steel" Carnegie\nCarnegie was named after A. Carnegie, the American industrialist known as the "King of Steel." By the great sire Sadler's Wells out of Arc winner Detroit, he was a blueblood, born in Ireland and trained in France.\nHe debuted late, in March 1994 at 3, and won first time out. But he then lost twice, and gave up on classics like the French Derby. Improving from summer, he won a listed race and then his first graded race, the G2 Prix Eugene Adam.\nIn autumn he aimed at the Arc and took on the Prix Niel prep. The favorite was French Derby winner Celtic Arms, with Carnegie 2nd choice. He turned in 6th, unleashed his kick to lead with 200m left and won by a length and a half.\nThen the Arc, with stars like King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. winner King's Theatre and Italian Derby winner White Muzzle. King's Theatre and others raced up front while Carnegie sat in a good midfield spot. In the straight he swept past the tiring leaders to take the lead, then held off the late chargers by a short head. Sire-and-son Arc winners had happened before, but this was the first-ever dam-and-son double.\n\n* The Growth of Hong Kong Racing\nThoroughbred racing took hold in Hong Kong from the 1960s under British rule. After Sha Tin Racecourse opened in 1978, Hong Kong racing grew fast, and in 1988 its first international race, the Hong Kong Invitation Cup, was founded.\nIn 1994 the Hong Kong International Vase, a new 2400m international race, began, and excitement grew further. In time the Hong Kong International Races became a festival of four G1s on one day: the Vase, the Sprint, the Mile and the Cup.\nToday Hong Kong even sells bets on which jockeys and trainers will do best in some races - its racing has evolved its own brand of entertainment.
