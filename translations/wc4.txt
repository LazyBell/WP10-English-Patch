32=* Mill Reef: First-Ever European Triple Crown\nRacing goes back to ancient Greece, but "modern racing" - run at racecourses under formal rules - is said to have begun in 16th-century England. It spread across Europe and later to America and Asia.\nAs races developed around the world, by the mid-20th century three stood out as Europe's biggest: the Derby (founded 1780), the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S. (1951) and the Arc (1920). Winning all three, arguably the world's top races, in one year is called the "European Triple Crown" in Japan, but from 1951 no horse managed it for a long time.\nIn 1970 the British colt Mill Reef won 5 of 6 at under a mile (1600m). In 1971 he tried his first mile in the 2000 Guineas and was 2nd (no shame - the winner was Brigadier Gerard, unbeatable at a mile), and since his sire Never Bend was a speed line, many expected him to stick to shorter races. Instead his camp stepped up to the 2400m Derby. Despite many doubts, he won clearly by 2 lengths. He then beat older horses by 6 lengths in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S., breaking Ribot's 5-length record for the biggest winning margin in the race's history.\nThe year before, Triple Crown winner Nijinsky had run in the St. Leger before the Arc and lost, so Mill Reef's camp gave him over two months' rest before the Arc. He won by 3 lengths in course-record time for Longchamp's 2400m. Mill Reef carved his name into history as the first European Triple Crown winner.\n\n* The Legendary 2000 Guineas\nBrigadier Gerard was a British colt named after Brigadier Gerard, hero of Conan Doyle's historical novels. Though he won all 4 starts at 2, the public's eyes were on two other stars of his crop, Mill Reef and My Swallow.\nIn 1971, at 3, his first race was the 2000 Guineas, the first leg of the British Triple Crown. With the crop's three best in the field, many rivals stayed away, and only 6 ran - the smallest field of the 20th century. My Swallow led with Mill Reef tracking him, but in the straight Brigadier Gerard unleashed an astonishing kick and won by 3 lengths. It is still remembered as "the greatest 2000 Guineas ever."\nBrigadier Gerard won his first 15 starts unbeaten until losing to Roberto in the Benson & Hedges Gold C. the next year, becoming the miler of the century and an idol of British racing fans. In time he was called "Britain's national hero," as hugely popular as the hero of the novels.
33=* Mill Reef Sets a European Record of 6 Straight G1 Wins\nThe year before, Mill Reef had won the first-ever European Triple Crown. Believing he still had room to grow, his camp kept him in training at 4 with a second Arc as the goal. In his first public workout of the year he ran 12 furlongs (2400m) and beat a stablemate who had been given a head start by fully 12 lengths, stunning the crowd.\nHe started in France in the Prix Ganay. The overwhelming favorite at 1.1, he exceeded even those hopes, winning by 10 lengths. Yuji Nohira, who rode another horse in the race, later praised him: "His build was nothing special - I thought we could produce one like that ourselves. But once he raced, he was something else. It may be rude to his jockey, but I thought anyone could have won on him."\nNext, back in England, he ran in the Coronation C. Few camps wanted to face him, so only 4 ran. Again favorite at 1.1, he seemed sure to dominate. But in the straight he was locked in a duel with Homeric and only edged ahead near the line to win by a neck. A hard-fought win, but it was his 6th straight G1, a European record.\nStill, his camp wasn't happy with the performance. A post-race check found a viral illness, and he had to skip the Benson & Hedges Gold C., where a rematch with Brigadier Gerard was planned (Brigadier Gerard suffered the only loss of his career in that race). The virus came back and leg problems followed, and just when a return seemed possible, he fractured a leg in training before the Arc. The injury was serious enough that he might have been put down, but treatment saved his life and he recovered enough to walk.\nHe never raced again, and the dream of a second Arc faded. But because his life was saved, he went to stud and later became leading sire. Even after leaving the turf, Mill Reef's name lived on in racing.\n\n* Secretariat, Horse of the Year at 2\nSecretariat was an American colt whose name means an administrative office. His camp had applied to the Jockey Club for many names, but all were rejected for various reasons, so the farm secretary handling the paperwork applied for "Secretariat" because she had once worked at one - and it was accepted. An unusual story.\nIn 1972 he debuted at 2 in a dirt maiden race. As his later nickname "Big Red" suggests, his size was remarkable, and he was favorite. He bumped another horse at the start, lost ground and finished 4th, but what followed was astonishing. In his next maiden he won by 4 lengths. After that, even when he started slowly, he made up for it with amazing late runs, finishing his 2yo year with 7 wins from 9 (one loss was a disqualification for interference).\nThe Eclipse Awards had begun the year before, when Ack Ack was named Horse of the Year. Secretariat was not only named champion 2yo colt but also Horse of the Year, ahead of La Prevoyante, a 2yo filly unbeaten in 12, and Riva Ridge, the 3yo Kentucky Derby winner. Some argued La Prevoyante deserved it on record, but so many had been captivated by Secretariat's thrilling late runs that he rose to the top of American racing.
34=* Allez France vs Dahlia\nThis year mares stood out in Europe. Above all, the clash of two of the same crop who would go down as historic greats - Allez France and Dahlia - thrilled racing. Both were French-trained fillies. Allez France swept the French fillies' Triple Crown - the Poule d'Essai des Pouliches, the Prix de Diane and the Prix Vermeille - and was 2nd in the Arc. Dahlia was 3rd, 2nd and 5th in those races, but became the first 3yo filly to win the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S., by a record 6 lengths. She also became the first filly to win the Washington DC International in America, and won the Irish Oaks too. Allez France finished ahead in all 8 of their meetings, but Dahlia was also highly rated for beating colts in big races abroad, and it was hard to say which was better.\nIn fact the two were linked even before their debuts: Maurice Zilber, who bought Allez France as agent for her owner, was then hired by Dahlia's connections as her private trainer before she raced. Zilber said Allez France was "ugly," and indeed she had a rugged, colt-like build, while Dahlia was said to be beautiful and elegant.\nOverlooked behind these two greats was another filly of the crop who shone in Britain: Mysterious. She won the 1000 Guineas and the Oaks, the British fillies' double, plus the Yorkshire Oaks. She then met Dahlia in the Irish Oaks but was beaten 3 lengths into 2nd.\nKalamoun, whose grandson would be the great sire Tony Bin, was another star of this crop, winning three G1s - the Poule d'Essai des Poulains, the Prix Lupin and the Prix Jacques le Marois - and showing rare talent.\nThis was also the year legendary British jockey L. Piggott first won the Arc. At the time French horses won almost every Arc, and foreign-trained winners were rare. Piggott, Britain's champion jockey, rode in it nearly every year, but fell just short in 14 tries, finally winning at 37 this year aboard Rheingold.\n\n\n* Secretariat's Astonishing 31-Length Win\n1973 was the year the first oil shock caused stagflation and dealt a heavy blow to the world economy. In America, as if to blow away the gloom, good news came from racing: Secretariat, Horse of the Year at 2 the year before, won the first US Triple Crown in 25 years with overwhelming power.\nHe won the Kentucky Derby and the Preakness S. in record time, and in the Belmont S. he left the field far behind, winning by 31 lengths in a staggering world-record 2:24.0. The record still stands, and no one has even run in the 2:25s since. News of this unprecedented Triple Crown winner reached even people who knew nothing of racing and gave the country hope. With frustration at politicians high over the Vietnam War and Watergate, some joked that "if Secretariat ran for president, he'd win" - a sign of his popularity and influence.\nAlso, two years after Europe adopted its Group system, the US and Canada introduced graded races from this year. Japan followed in 1984.
