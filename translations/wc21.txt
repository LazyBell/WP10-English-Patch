111=* Teio vs McQueen: Their First and Last Showdown\nAfter his Tenno Sho (Autumn) demotion the year before, Mejiro McQueen kept losing: 4th in the Japan Cup, 2nd in the Arima Kinen. But he opened this year with a 5-length win in the Hanshin Daishoten. In the Tenno Sho (Spring), going for a repeat, he faced the year-younger genius Tokai Teio, back from a 10-month layoff.\nMcQueen, famed as the best stayer, against Teio, still unbeaten after winning the Osaka Hai on his return. The clash of the two who carried the second boom after Oguri Cap's retirement was billed "the showdown of the century." Over 3200m at Kyoto, McQueen's record made him look the better bet. But Teio's jockey Yukio Okabe, usually measured, said Teio "could run to the ends of the earth." Not to be outdone, McQueen's jockey Yutaka Take replied, "Mine will climb to heaven," fueling the hype.\nTeio was favorite and McQueen 2nd - the first time McQueen wasn't favorite since Take began riding him. McQueen sat in a good spot with Teio glued to his left flank. McQueen kicked from the third turn and Teio chased, but faded in the straight. McQueen won easily; Teio was 5th. The showdown ended with McQueen the first horse ever to win the Tenno Sho (Spring) twice in a row.\n\n\n\n* The Assassin Who Stopped an Unbeaten Triple Crown\nThe hill course built at Ritto in 1985 was influential enough to flip the East-West balance. The most famous horse it produced was Mihono Bourbon, "child of the hill course." Put through hard hill training by trainer Tameo Toyama, known for Spartan methods, he gained rare speed.\nIn 1992, unbeaten, he led all the way to win the Spring S. and the Satsuki Sho. In the Japanese Derby he drew wide (gate 15), bad for a front-runner, but grabbed the lead and kept it, beating Rice Shower by 4 lengths.\nThen the Kikuka Sho, unbeaten. With Tokai Teio having just missed an unbeaten Triple Crown the year before, hopes were sky-high. Kyoei Bowgun, who had vowed to lead, went to the front. Mihono Bourbon, who had led in every race that year, fought his rider in this new situation. He finally took the lead after the third turn, but Rice Shower surged past in the straight. With nothing left to fight back, he was 2nd, missing the unbeaten Triple Crown.\nHe was injured before the Japan Cup, newly made an international G1. He tried to return but kept getting hurt, and the double classic winner called "child of the hill course" and "the precision machine" retired, to much regret.
112=* The Dramas of B, N and W\nThis year's classics were seen as a two-horse fight: Biwa Hayahide, ridden by Yukio Okabe, and Winning Ticket, ridden by Masato Shibata. In their first meeting, the Satsuki Sho, Winning Ticket passed the forward-running Biwa Hayahide, only for Biwa Hayahide to retake the lead in the straight. It seemed settled - until Narita Taishin flew from last and widest to win by a neck. Ridden by Yutaka Take, he had been a distant third on record. After this the classics were seen as a Big Three, dubbed "BNW" from their initials.\nIn the Japanese Derby, BNW's odds were tight at 3.6 to 4.0. Shibata, aboard favorite Winning Ticket, had won many big races but never the Derby, and wanted it more than anyone. Usually happy to talk to the press, he refused interviews the week before. He stayed on the rail the others avoided, broke clear in the straight and won a duel with the charging Biwa Hayahide. Winning Ticket was a "one-way ticket to victory," giving Shibata his first Derby.\n2nd in both spring classics, Biwa Hayahide's camp took two steps to win the Kikuka Sho. Deciding the quick acceleration he lacked against his rivals, they added hill training as with Mihono Bourbon, and he gained the solid frame that powers acceleration. Then they removed his ear covers. Sensitive to noise, he had worn them since his debut, but they took the risk to sharpen his response when it counted.\nIn the Kikuka Sho he sat near the front and led at the final turn. In the straight he showed a burst that left Winning Ticket, who was tracking him, far behind, winning by 5 lengths in Japanese record time - a reward for the efforts of horse and camp.\nThis year's classics ended with each of BNW winning one - a true Big Three.\n\n\n* The "King's" Miracle Comeback\nThe year before, Tokai Teio won the Japan Cup like his sire, but was a shock 11th in the Arima Kinen. This year he aimed for the Takarazuka Kinen, but suffered his third fracture and went on another long break. Most thought "we'll never see the King of old again," but his camp didn't give up. Trainer Shoichi Matsumoto later said, "I knew that if we got him fully ready, he could show his power straight away."\nHis comeback was the Arima Kinen, his first race in a year. With 8 G1 winners in a star field, including Kikuka Sho winner Biwa Hayahide and Derby winner Winning Ticket, he was only 4th choice. He sat in a good midfield spot, broke clear at the final turn and drew level with Biwa Hayahide in the straight, winning the fierce duel to the line. Winning the Arima Kinen after 364 days off was called "the miracle comeback," and it remains the longest layoff before a G1 win.\n\n* "Weekly Gallop" Launches\nThis year the racing magazine "Weekly Gallop" was launched as a special edition of Sankei Sports. Its first cover featured Mejiro McQueen, hot favorite for that week's Tenno Sho (Autumn) (sadly scratched with leg trouble). Its big selling point was color photos, details and reviews of Sunday's main race on sale the very next day, while the excitement was still fresh. Covering JRA, local and overseas racing, with columns by active jockeys Yukio Okabe and Masayoshi Ebina and trainer Yuji Nohira from the start, it became a must-read and is still popular today.
113=* Chasing His Brother to the Triple Crown\nNarita Brian drew attention as half-brother to the previous Kikuka Sho winner Biwa Hayahide, but his early form was erratic. Unlike his brother, who won the Kikuka Sho after his ear covers came off, he was so timid he feared his own shadow, so he was fitted with a shadow roll - and then won the Kyoto Sansai S. and the Asahi Hai Sansai S. in brilliant style, becoming champion 2yo colt.\nIn spring 1994 he kept winning easily. He won the Satsuki Sho in course-record time and the Japanese Derby as a huge favorite with 61.8% of win bets. After Tokai Teio in '91 and Mihono Bourbon in '92 had both just missed the Triple Crown, fans longed to see one. All their hopes rested on Narita Brian.\nBut he fell ill in summer. Undertrained, he was 2nd in the Kyoto Shimbun Hai. Fans still trusted him, making him favorite at 1.7 for the Kikuka Sho. As Steel Cast set off in a huge lead like his dam, Brian waited calmly in midfield. In the straight he pulled 7 lengths clear to win in a race-record time, 0.1 seconds faster than his brother's record the year before - the 5th Triple Crown winner ever and the first in 10 years, since Symboli Rudolf.\nHe won his three classics by a total of 15 lengths. With no rival left in his crop, fans hoped for a clash with his brother on the older horses' main path, but Biwa Hayahide's injury in the Tenno Sho (Autumn) ended that dream. In the Arima Kinen, without his brother, Brian took the lead early at the final turn and won, keeping stars like Hishi Amazon and Rice Shower at bay.\n\n* Hishi Amazon: From the Back Roads to the Top Mare\nFrom 1984, "mochikomi" (foals born from mares imported in foal, or imported with their dams before age 1) could run in the classics. But "foreign-bred" horses, bred abroad and registered in Japan, still couldn't.\nThat unavoidable wall blocked Hishi Amazon. She drew attention by winning the Hanshin Sansai Himba S., but as a foreign-bred she couldn't run in the classics. As if channeling her frustration into her running, she won 5 graded races in a row. Then in the Queen Elizabeth II Cup, a G1 she could run in, she met Oaks winner Chokai Carol. After a fierce duel down the straight she won by a nose, showing the pride of a queen forced onto the back roads. With 6 straight graded wins, she was named champion 3yo filly.\n\n* Sakura Bakushin O, a Born Sprinter\nWith a first-class classic pedigree, Sakura Bakushin O carried big hopes. But after a heavy defeat in 12th in the Spring S., a Satsuki Sho trial, his camp gave up on the classics and moved him to sprints to use his speed.\nThere he showed his true worth, winning the Sprinters S. in 1993 and '94 and dominating the sprint division, and was named champion sprinter in '94. He won 11 of 12 at 1400m or less, but 0 of 9 beyond 1400m - a true born sprinter.\nHe also succeeded at stud, passing his overwhelming speed to his offspring.
114=* The Great Sire Sunday Silence\nSunday Silence went unsold as a youngster for his awkward looks, but as a racehorse he won 6 G1s, including two legs of the US Triple Crown and the BC Classic, and was Eclipse Horse of the Year. But his success story didn't end there.\nRetiring in 1990, he was imported by Shadai Farm to stand in Japan. His sire Halo was a great horse, but his dam Wishing Well was no blueblood. With that pedigree, his sire's fiery temper, and his failure to sell for much at 2, American breeders scoffed that he'd never succeed at stud, and at first he wasn't rated highly in Japan either.\nBut his first crop, including Fuji Kiseki, did well in 1994, and in '95 Genuine and Dance Partner won classics, and he shot to leading sire - the fastest ever in Japan, with just two crops. His offspring kept succeeding, and he was leading sire 13 years running until 2007. In that time Still in Love won the fillies' Triple Crown in 2003 and Deep Impact the Triple Crown in 2005, making him the first sire of both a colt and a filly Triple Crown winner.\nWith stars among his grandchildren and great-grandchildren too, he redrew the bloodlines of Japanese thoroughbreds, and his blood keeps spreading worldwide. It's fair to say Sunday Silence alone raised the level of Japanese racing.\n\n* Japanese Horses Abroad\nThe Japan Cup, founded in 1981 under the motto "breed horses that can compete with the world," helped raise Japanese horses' level by letting them face foreign rivals. But since they could meet foreign horses without traveling, it also made Japanese racing reluctant to race abroad. Some, like Sirius Symboli and Gallop Dyna, tried, but none won.\nWhen the Hong Kong International Races began taking shape from '88, interest in racing abroad grew. In '95 Fujiyama Kenzan finally won the Hong Kong International Cup (local G1, international G2) - the first Japanese win in a major flat race abroad in 36 years, since Hakuchikara made the first postwar trip.\n\n* Rice Shower Falls at Yodo\nRice Shower, "the black assassin from Kanto," won fame as a villain for denying Mihono Bourbon an unbeaten Triple Crown in 1992 and Mejiro McQueen a third straight Tenno Sho (Spring) in '93. But after that Tenno Sho he slumped, as if burned out.\nIn '95 he returned to the Tenno Sho (Spring) after two years, only 4th choice despite being a past winner. In a surprise attack he launched a long run from the third turn, took the lead and won by a nose. Fans rejoiced at his comeback - not as a villain, but as a hero who fought on after his old rivals had gone.\nBut tragedy struck in his next race, the Takarazuka Kinen. Mid-race he suffered a dislocation and collapsed. Judged beyond saving on the spot, he was put down. By fate, that year's Takarazuka Kinen was held at Kyoto, scene of his comeback. The tragic end of the great stayer at Yodo left racing in deep grief.
115=* Queen of the Sand, Hokuto Vega\nHokuto Vega won the 1993 Queen Elizabeth II Cup, but after that did little beyond winning the Sapporo Kinen, then a G3. But after winning the Empress Hai at Kawasaki in '95 by 18 lengths, she switched to dirt full-time the next year, and her winning run began. In her first race, the Kawasaki Kinen, against elites like Lively Mount, then seen as the best dirt horse, and '93 Japan Cup winner Legacy World, she won by 5 lengths. She kept winning JRA-local graded races, and for her 5th, the Teio Sho, over 70,000 fans - far beyond Oi's capacity - came to see her. As if her turf struggles had never happened, she won all 8 of her dirt races that year and was named champion dirt horse.\nNo one then imagined that two years after this brilliant switch to dirt, tragedy would strike her in Dubai.\n\n* Shinzan Passes Away\nIn 1964 Shinzan became the second Triple Crown winner ever and the first postwar, and also won the Tenno Sho (Autumn) and Arima Kinen to be called the five-crown winner. So dominant was he that "Beyond Shinzan" became Japanese racing's motto, the goal for horsemen for 20 years until unbeaten Triple Crown winner Symboli Rudolf. As a sire he produced classic winners like double winner Miho Shinzan and Kikuka Sho winner Minagawa Manna, and set a record of winners in 24 straight years - a light in an era when Japanese-bred sires struggled.\nWhile many racehorses give their all and die young, Shinzan lived very long after retiring from stud. In '95 he set the Japanese longevity record for a thoroughbred, then for all light horses (breeds improved for speed, including thoroughbreds), and this year he died at 35, much mourned.\n\n* The "Brilliant Class 12" Debuts\nThis year the rookie jockeys called "the brilliant Class 12" of the racing school debuted. Leading them was Yuichi Fukunaga, son of great jockey Yoichi Fukunaga, who won his first two rides. Others included Ryuji Wada, later regular rider of T.M. Opera O; 5 of the 10 who debuted went on to win G1s.\nClass 12 also included the JRA's first three female jockeys. One of them, Junko Hosoe, is still active today as a racing analyst ("horse collaborator").
116=* Air Groove: First Female Horse of the Year in 26 Years\nAir Groove won the Oaks the year before to become the best filly of her crop. In the new Shuka Sho she was too keyed up and finished 10th, and a fracture found afterwards kept her out a long time. Returning this year as an older mare, she won the Mermaid S. easily, then beat Satsuki Sho winner Genuine with ease in the Sapporo Kinen. In autumn she seemed headed for the Queen Elizabeth II Cup, reborn the year before as a fillies' and mares' race open to older horses, but her camp believed she could match the colts and chose the Tenno Sho (Autumn), her first G1 against males.\nShe was 2nd choice behind last year's winner Bubble Gum Fellow. Silence Suzuka set a fast pace in a big lead, with Bubble Gum Fellow near the front and Air Groove in midfield. In the straight, as Bubble Gum Fellow went to lead, Air Groove came powerfully alongside on the outside; they fought a dead heat from 200m out, and she won by a neck. It was the first win by a female in 17 years, since Pretty Cast in '80, and the first since the race was cut to 2000m in '84. She was then 2nd in the Japan Cup (ahead of Bubble Gum Fellow in 3rd again) and 3rd in the Arima Kinen, matching the best colts, and came to be called "the Empress." That year she was named Horse of the Year, the first female since Tomei in 1971, 26 years earlier, and reigned as the best mare.\n\n* Mayano Top Gun Wins the Big Three Clash\nIn 1995 Mayano Top Gun won the Kikuka Sho and Arima Kinen and was named champion 3yo colt. He won the Takarazuka Kinen the next year, but then was 4th in the All Comers and 7th in the Arima Kinen, and some said he was finished.\nIn '97 his camp kept him racing but decided he'd retire at year's end, targeting the Tenno Sho (Spring). He was seen as a front-runner, as when he led all the way in the '95 Arima Kinen. But jockey Seiki Tabara noticed he no longer wanted to go forward after the start, and in the Hanshin Daishoten prep let him do as he liked, waiting at the very back. He ran away from the field in the straight for his first win in six months. Many still said "Top Gun is at his best up front," but Tabara believed putting the horse's feelings first meant better running.\nThe Tenno Sho (Spring) drew Sakura Laurel and Marvelous Sunday, who both beat him in the last Arima Kinen. Billed as a Big Three clash, Sakura Laurel, going for a repeat, was favorite, followed by Top Gun and Marvelous Sunday, with tight odds. Tabara let Top Gun settle at the back as he wished. As the other two broke clear early and dueled in the straight, Top Gun unleashed an astonishing kick from far back and wide, from where it seemed impossible, caught both just before the line and won by over a length - in Japanese (and world) record time for 3200m.\nAs it turned out, this was the Big Three's final clash. The win, achieved with Tabara and Top Gun as one, is etched as one of the great races in Japanese history.\n\n* Dirt Graded Races Established\nWith JRA-local racing exchange in full swing from 1995, this year JRA-local dirt graded races were renamed "dirt graded races." The JRA's February S. and local races like the Tokyo Daishoten (Oi) and Nambu Hai (Morioka) were made G1, new races like the Hokkaido Sprint Cup (G3, Sapporo) and Marine Cup (G3, Funabashi) were created, and a unified G1-G3 system covered local exchange races too. The February S., newly a G1, crowned Shinko Windy as its first champion. In the first Teio Sho (Oi) as a G1, Concert Boy and Abukuma Poro showed local pride with a local 1-2.\nNationwide grading boosted JRA-local exchange and greatly helped dirt racing develop.
