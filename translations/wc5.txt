35=* "Queen of Longchamp" Allez France\nTrue to her name, "Go, France," Allez France had swept the French fillies' Triple Crown (Poule d'Essai des Pouliches, Prix de Diane, Prix Vermeille) the year before and become France's leading mare. But in the Arc, Europe's greatest race, she had tasted bitter defeat in 2nd.\nIn 1974, at 4, she began in the G2 Prix d'Harcourt and won easily. She took two straight G1s, won the G3 Prix Foy prep without a scare, and headed to the Arc for revenge.\nAll seemed set, but 10 days before the race her regular jockey Yves Saint-Martin fell and broke his hip. A substitute was lined up, but Saint-Martin refused to give up the ride and took painkillers to get in the saddle. She was expected to come from behind as usual, but from the final turn she swept past the field and hit the front early - perhaps Saint-Martin avoided a late charge for fear of his injury. Pressed by others near the line, she just held on to win by a head. As a daughter of Sea-Bird, often called France's greatest horse, she completed a father-daughter Arc double, to huge applause.\nThat year Allez France won all 5 of her starts at Longchamp and was named France's champion older mare and Horse of the Year. With 12 of her 13 career wins at Longchamp, fans came to call her "the Queen of Longchamp."\n\n* "The Black Butterfly Dancing Around the World" Dahlia\nThe year before, the French filly Dahlia had stunned fans by winning the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S. by a record 6 lengths. She also traveled to America and became the first filly to win the G1 Washington DC International, making her name known worldwide.\nThis year she opened in the Prix d'Harcourt, her 5th meeting with rival Allez France, and lost by six and a half lengths. In the Prix Ganay she was well beaten again, 5th behind her rival. Traveling to England for the Coronation C., she was only 3rd - three straight losses, a poor start.\nBut back in France, in the G1 Grand Prix de Saint-Cloud, with Allez France absent and a weaker field, she won convincingly. Dahlia then flew back to England for the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S. Despite a strong field including Highclere, winner of the 1000 Guineas and Prix de Diane, she cruised out of the back of midfield to become the first horse ever to win the race twice.\nShe kept racing around the world, winning 10 G1s in her career. At a time when shipping horses abroad was still far from routine, she raced in six countries with great success - truly one of the horses who opened the road for international racing.
36=* Star Appeal's Arc Challenge\nStar Appeal was born at the Irish branch of Germany's Rottgen Stud. He debuted in Ireland and won 5 of 13, a decent record, before moving to Germany. His first race there, and last of his 3yo season, was the Arc. Among Europe's stars like Allez France and Dahlia, he was naturally the outsider - least fancied of 27. He finished 13th, far behind winner Rheingold, clearly outclassed. The next year he won two G2s but no G1, and his 4yo year was a slump with 2 wins from 14.\nIn 1975, at 5, his camp kept trying G1s. He traveled to Italy for the Gran Premio di Milano, a high-class race with G1 winners like Dahlia - and he won it, his first G1. Next came England's Eclipse S. Even as a G1 winner his record was thin, and he was only 6th choice at 21, but he defied the odds and won.\nTwo straight G1s brought sudden attention, but he then lost three G1s in a row. His stock fell sharply, and in the Arc he was least fancied of 24 at 120.7. He trailed at the very back. Into the final turn he split the pack right down the middle, took the lead in mid-straight and won by 3 lengths. The 2nd was 13th choice, while last year's winner Allez France was 5th - a huge upset. Star Appeal is still the only horse to win the Arc as the least-fancied runner.\nIt was also the first Arc win by a German-trained horse, and German fans and media went wild. The excitement was immense: an Irish-born horse, trained in Germany and ridden by a British jockey, winning in France - the French president even remarked that "this is a victory for all of Europe."\n\n* Grundy Wins Britain's Race of the Century\nThe British colt Grundy won a G1 unbeaten in 4 starts at 2 and carried high hopes. In 1975, at 3, a stablemate kicked him in the face and broke his nose, and he struggled to win for a while, but then took three straight classics in two countries - the Irish 2000 Guineas, the Derby and the Irish Derby - and was confirmed as the best of his crop.\nAmong the 4yos, the British colt Bustino drew attention after winning the Coronation C. in course-record time for his 2nd straight G1. The record was 2 seconds faster than Grundy's winning time on the same course, and many fans thought he could beat Grundy.\nThey met in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S., renamed this year. Grundy was favorite, Bustino 2nd choice, and Dahlia, going for a 3rd straight win, 3rd choice. Two pacemakers from Bustino's camp went to the front - a plan to set a fast pace so Bustino, the faster on the clock, would have the edge. It worked: the pace was blistering and Bustino took the lead. Grundy was about 3 lengths behind, but 2 furlongs (about 400m) out he surged level and went ahead with a furlong left. It seemed over, but Bustino fought back with amazing grit. The breathless duel went on, and at the last moment Grundy got half a length in front at the line. The time, 2:26.98, broke the race record by over 2.5 seconds and stood until 2010.\nHow fierce the duel was can be seen from the fact that both horses left the turf within two months. The race was called "The Race of the Century" and is still talked about by fans today.
37=* The Greatest Handicap Horse Ever\nPerhaps because American racing holds that "a truly great horse wins even while giving weight to others," there are still many G1 handicaps in the US. Back then horses were often assigned 130 lbs (about 59 kg) or more. Forego kept fighting through that adversity.\nForego debuted in 1973. Even as a foal he had a huge appetite - when hungry, he reportedly tried to eat people's hands. As a result he grew even bigger than Secretariat, the famous giant of his crop. In his debut year at 3 he ran an amazing 18 races. When Triple Crown winner Secretariat retired that year, Forego rose as if to take his place, piling up wins and ever-higher weights. At 4 he won a G1 or G2 at 12, 7 and 16 furlongs in a row - a feat no other horse could match. That year he was named Eclipse Horse of the Year, champion older horse and champion sprinter. At 5 he shrugged off still higher weights to win 4 G1s, again taking Horse of the Year and champion older horse.\nIn 1976, at 6, he didn't start until May, but nothing could stop him; he won the G1 Metropolitan H., which he had lost twice before. In his last race of the year, the G1 Marlboro Cup Invitational H., he won carrying 137 lbs (about 62 kg). With 4 G1 wins that year he was Horse of the Year and champion older horse for the third straight year.\nHe raced until 8, finishing with 34 wins from 57 starts. Those numbers suggest a very sound horse, but he actually suffered from chronic leg trouble. Even so, the sight of him winning under huge weights was spectacular, and fans call him "the greatest handicap horse of all time."\n\n* "The Puerto Rican Fugitive" Bold Forbes\nSince 1983 racing countries and regions have been graded Part I to Part III under standards set by the International Cataloguing Standards Committee (ICSC). Puerto Rico is currently Part II, and its famous horses include Galgo Jr., world record holder with 137 career wins, and Camarero, who won 73 of 77.\nBold Forbes was a colt born in America who debuted in Puerto Rico in 1975. He won his debut by 17 lengths and went on to win 5 straight. His owner believed he was good enough for a bigger stage and moved him to America, aiming for the Kentucky Derby.\nIn 1976, at 3, he didn't always win at first, but he quickly improved, won three graded stakes in a row and went into the Kentucky Derby. The favorite was Honest Pleasure, the previous year's champion 2yo colt, on a 9-race winning streak; Bold Forbes was 2nd choice. He broke sharply and set a fast pace in a big lead. The others closed gradually, but he never tired and beat Honest Pleasure by a length. In the Preakness S. he again went for a big lead, but Honest Pleasure pressed him hard, the pace fell apart, and he faded in the straight to finish 3rd.\nThen came the third classic, the Belmont S. Crowds of Latino fans packed Belmont Park, waving Puerto Rican flags and cheering him on. Bold Forbes again set off in a big lead. In the straight the others drew level, but as if carried by the roar of the crowd he held on by a neck. He had won two legs of the US Triple Crown, to thunderous applause.
