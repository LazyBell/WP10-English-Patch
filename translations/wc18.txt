93=* Fated Rivals and a Third Assassin\nThe previous year's Horse of the Year was double classic winner Take Hope, while Haiseiko received a special "People's Award" - proof he was a true national idol. In summer 1973, while both were resting, a horse returned from a 20-month layoff after surviving a life-threatening illness: Tanino Chikara. Half-brother to double classic winner Tanino Moutiers, he had been watched since before his debut but had just one win. Running with a unique low-headed style, he won race after race all the way to the Tenno Sho (Autumn). Just four months after his return, a "power crawling along the ground" had broken into the Big Two.\nHaiseiko and Take Hope, who had carried the racing boom, both announced the year-end Arima Kinen as their final race. Favorite was Take Hope, Tenno Sho (Spring) winner, who now finally topped Haiseiko in popularity too. Haiseiko, beaten in all 6 starts at 2400m or more, was 3rd choice, with Tanino Chikara 2nd. Kitano Kachidoki, winner of the Satsuki Sho and Kikuka Sho and that year's Horse of the Year, didn't run.\nTanino Chikara took the lead at the start. The year before he had watched Haiseiko too closely, couldn't catch the leaders and was 4th - this bold tactic was the lesson learned. Haiseiko closed from mid-race, Take Hope surged from behind, and it came down to three. In the straight Tanino Chikara pulled away from both and won by 5 lengths from Haiseiko - a rout, leading at his own pace. But all eyes were on the battle for 2nd and 3rd between Haiseiko and Take Hope, who had fought 9 epic battles. Fans felt an era ending with the retirement of the two who had led the boom.\n\n* "Single-Bracket" Designation, Mark of a Favorite\nBetting then was mainly bracket quinella, and if a favorite was scratched, bettors could only hope for the outsider in the same bracket. Haiseiko's huge support in the previous year's Japanese Derby exposed the problem, and in 1974 "single-bracket designation" (first called the seed system) was introduced, placing a horse expected to draw heavy betting alone in its bracket. It was first used in that year's Satsuki Sho, where unbeaten Kitano Kachidoki was given a single bracket and won.
94=* Battle of the Generation's Best Colt and Filly\nEven after Haiseiko, leader of the first racing boom, retired, attendance kept rising, and in 1975 JRA racing drew a record 15 million fans. The filly who drew them more than any was Tesco Gaby, famed for her overwhelming speed.\nFrom birth she was noted for a muscular build rare in a filly and a very calm temperament, and after debuting she won 4 straight with blazing front-running. In 1975 she kept rolling in the classics, leading all the way to win the Oka Sho easily. Fast Oka Sho winners often ran out of stamina in the Oaks, but she led all the way again with rare speed. Japanese racing then prized stamina - both Tenno Sho races were 3200m - but it gradually shifted toward speed, and some say Tesco Gaby, who swept the racing world with natural speed, was the turning point.\nAmong colts, Kabuto Yao drew attention for front-running so extreme it was called "madman's pace." Kicked by another horse as a youngster, he became a timid horse terrified of others, but his camp turned that to their advantage, training him to run fast from the front. Born healthy with superb heart and lungs, he grew into an elite front-runner. Years later jockey Yukio Okabe, a devotee of unbeaten Triple Crown winner Symboli Rudolf, said: "Against Kabuto Yao in his Derby, I don't know if even Rudolf could have won."\nBefore the classics, the two met once, in the Tokyo Yonsai S. (now the Kyodo News Hai), billed as a clash between two contrasting front-runners - one a hope since before her debut, the other awakened after his.\nTesco Gaby went forward at the start, but Kabuto Yao, refusing to yield the lead, shot out wide while fighting his rider. In that order they rounded the final turn. Mid-straight, as the field spread out, Kabuto Yao, hating the whip, veered so badly he almost hit Tesco Gaby. As he lost his balance she drew level, and a fierce duel followed, but Kabuto Yao held on by a neck. Both went on to win two classics each, proving their power. The race is remembered as the first and last great duel between two future double classic winners.\n\n* "Mr. Keiba" Hangs Up His Whip\nJockey Yuji Nohira achieved many feats - the first 100 wins in a year in JRA history, the first win abroad by a Japanese jockey - and was known for partnering Speed Symboli. This year he retired to take over his late father's stable, winning his farewell race, the Meguro Kinen. He never won a colts' classic (he won the Oka Sho and Oaks with filly Kane Keyaki).\nBut as a trainer he met the great Symboli Rudolf and won the unbeaten Triple Crown with him. For his great contribution to Japanese racing as both jockey and trainer, Nohira was called "Mr. Keiba."
95=* Epic Battle: "Pegasus" vs "Prince of the Blaze"\nMore than a year after Haiseiko left the turf, fans longed for a new star to fill the void. Then came the pair later called "TT."\nTosho Boy, called "Pegasus" for his flying stride, and Ten Point, the "Prince of the Blaze" for his beautiful chestnut coat and straight white blaze. Their clash gripped fans throughout this year's classics. Their first meeting was the Satsuki Sho, an East-West clash between Kansai's Ten Point and Kanto's Tosho Boy. Tosho Boy won by 5 lengths from Ten Point. Kansai courses then had few uphill straights compared with Kanto's. After the loss Ten Point's camp tearfully said, "Unless Ritto builds a hill course, we'll never beat Kanto horses" - which is said to have led to the hill training course at Ritto and today's Kansai dominance.\nTT's first battle was settled, but their rivalry went on. In the Japanese Derby and Kikuka Sho both lost to outsiders, but at year's end they went into the Arima Kinen as the stars against older greats like spring Tenno Sho winner Erimo George, with fillies' double winner Teitania also from their crop. Sitting just ahead of midfield behind the front-runners, TT closed on the lead at the final turn and slipped clear in the straight. Tosho Boy passed Erimo George first and held off Ten Point by a length and a half. It was the first Arima Kinen with 3yos 1st and 2nd. Winning the fated TT duel, Tosho Boy broke the race record by 1.1 seconds and was named Horse of the Year and champion 3yo colt.\nBut TT's battle wasn't over. The next year a new assassin joined, and it grew even fiercer.\n\n* New Ways to Enjoy Racing\nTelephone betting, trialed since 1974, was fully introduced this year: members could place bets by phone. Push-button phones were rare, so most bets were placed by telling an operator. Taken for granted now, betting from home was revolutionary then. It later evolved into voice-guided and internet betting, widening how fans enjoy racing.
96=* A Race for the Ages: TTG's Arima Kinen\nIn the previous year's Kikuka Sho, while all eyes were on TT and Derby winner Climb Kaiser, the winner was Green Grass, who had barely scraped in as the lowest earner in the field. A late bloomer with just one win during TT's spring classic battles, he crashed the TT rivalry as "the green assassin." The three became known as TTG and are still talked about.\nIn 1977 TTG, now older horses, clashed in the Takarazuka Kinen. Tosho Boy had been off since the previous Arima Kinen with leg trouble. Fresh off the break, his workouts were dull and his stable said he "lacked spirit," so he drifted and Ten Point was favorite. Ten Point had never beaten Tosho Boy - most fans thought his chance for revenge had come. But master jockey Kunihiko Take held Tosho Boy to a perfect slow pace in front, then ran the last 1000m faster than the then-record for 1000m on turf, winning with a long sustained run. After this race Ten Point declined an invitation to America's Washington DC International, vowing to "beat Tosho Boy" and become Japan's best.\nIn autumn Tosho Boy's camp announced he would retire after the Arima Kinen - Ten Point's last chance to beat his nemesis and TTG's final battle. Tosho Boy had the better record, but fans again made Ten Point favorite, as in the spring Grand Prix. TT broke clear early and fought a match race, neither giving up the lead - a clash of pride that set Nakayama roaring. The two stayed in front into the straight with no one near. Green Grass charged from behind, but TT kept going, and after a fierce duel Ten Point crossed the line three-quarters of a length ahead. Beating Tosho Boy at last, he was unanimously named Horse of the Year, the first unanimous winner in 21 years since Meiji Hikari.\nThis Arima Kinen, where TTG gave everything, is still hailed as the greatest race in Japanese history. Tosho Boy retired and TTG's era ended. In every race all three ran, they took 1st to 3rd - proof of how far above the rest they were.\n\n* Tragedy of the "Mochikomi"\nAs TTG thrilled the nation, an extraordinary horse was born a year younger: Maruzensky, later called "the supercar." By British Triple Crown winner Nijinsky out of a mare by US Hall of Famer Buckpasser, he was a blueblood "mochikomi."\nA mochikomi is a foal conceived abroad and born in Japan. Back then, Japanese stars like Speed Symboli and Takeshiba O were no match for foreign horses abroad, and Japan was thought far behind the world. So when trade was liberalized in 1971, to protect domestic breeders, mochikomi, like foreign-bred horses, were barred from the Big Eight races except the Arima Kinen.\nMaruzensky swept the racing world with overwhelming speed, and fans badly wanted him in the Japanese Derby. But despite desperate appeals from his jockey Nakanowatari, he never got the chance. He retired injured, unbeaten in 8. He passed his speed to his offspring and produced many G1 winners as a sire and broodmare sire, hugely influencing racing.\nFrom 1983 mochikomi were treated as domestic horses and the restriction ended. The regret of those who never saw Maruzensky run in the Derby may have paved the way for later mochikomi stars like Nishino Flower and Biwa Hayahide.
97=* Farewell, Ten Point\nThe year before, TTG had thrilled racing. Having finally beaten his nemesis Tosho Boy, who retired after the Arima Kinen, Ten Point set out this year on his long-planned trip abroad. But many Kansai fans wanted to see him once more before he left, so his camp entered him in the Nihon Keizai Shinshun Hai (now the Nikkei Shinshun Hai) at Kyoto. Everyone believed it would be a send-off win.\nIn falling snow, carrying a massive 66.5 kg, Ten Point led from the start down the backstretch. Erimo George and others pressed him, but just as everyone thought he was in a different class, he suddenly dropped back before the final turn. It was a severe fracture. Though doctors said it was hopeless, desperate treatment continued, but 43 days later Ten Point passed away.\nHis tragic death was widely reported, bringing grief to racing and raising many issues, such as whether to assign excessively heavy weights. Two days after his death a farewell ceremony was held at Ritto Training Center - the first funeral ever held for a thoroughbred in Japan.\nFarewell, Ten Point... Shuji Terayama, a poet and playwright who loved racing, dedicated a poem to him (in "Tabiji no Hate"), and many fans grieved.\n\n* Hatsushiba O, Toward an Unprecedented Fourth Crown\nSouthern Kanto's classics, the spring Haneda Hai and Tokyo Derby plus the Tokyo Okan Sho founded in autumn 1964, were modeled on the JRA's European-style classics (first two legs in spring, third in autumn).\nThis year Southern Kanto's star was Hatsushiba O, son of Takeshiba O, who ran on turf or dirt at any distance. He won the Haneda Hai and Tokyo Derby in spring, then the Tokyo Okan Sho by 6 lengths in autumn, the third Southern Kanto triple crown winner ever.\nBut he didn't stop. Aiming for a fourth crown that the two previous triple crown winners (Hikaru Takai and Golden Ribot) never managed, he ran in the year-end Tokyo Daishoten and won by two and a half lengths, the first Southern Kanto quadruple crown ever. The European-style format lasted until 1995, but only two others won all four (San Oi in 1983 and Rosita in 1989).\n\n* Miho Training Center Opens\nJapanese racing originally did everything at racecourses nationwide, from development and training to racing. But as racing's popularity and horse numbers grew, racecourse stables neared their limits, and urban sprawl around tracks made keeping horses there harder. So "training centers" dedicated to training and developing racehorses were planned.\nIn Kansai a site was found smoothly and Ritto opened in 1969, but in Kanto choosing and buying land dragged on. Finally, 17 years after it was planned, Miho opened in 1978. With both centers open, the East-West battle intensified and racing developed further.
98=* Avenging His Sire in the Derby\nKatsurano Haiseiko, from the first crop of idol horse Haiseiko, who sparked the first racing boom, carried great hopes. Leg trouble meant it took him 4 starts to break his maiden, but he won 3 straight at 3 and joined the classics. He was narrowly 2nd in the Satsuki Sho, where a father-son double was hoped for, then went into the Japanese Derby - the race where his sire lost to Take Hope - as favorite.\nWith well over 20 runners in the Derby then, horses left behind at the start had little chance, and a top-10 spot at the first turn was called the "Derby position." He settled right at the edge, in 10th. He moved up to 3rd at the final turn and burst clear in the straight. Drawing alongside came Rindo Purban - who, as fate would have it, had won the Yonsai Chukyori S. like Take Hope, and was ridden by Isao Shimada, the very jockey who beat Haiseiko with Take Hope in the Derby. But as if shaking off that fate, Katsurano Haiseiko showed his pride and won by a nose.\nHis fine run avenging his sire thrilled many fans. In an era dominated by imported sires, it was the first Derby win by a son of a Japanese-bred sire in 20 years, since Komatsu Hikari (by Tosa Midori) in 1959.\n\n* TTG's Last Stand\nGreen Grass, one of the TTG Big Three, often fell short compared with TT and was seen as the plain one. But after TT left the turf he kept running alone as No. 3, winning the Tenno Sho (Spring) the year before. This year, at 7 (old age system), he rested after two spring races, ran once in autumn, and with chronic leg trouble chose his third Arima Kinen (3rd and 6th before) as his farewell - the year-end showpiece his rivals had won (Tosho Boy in 1976, Ten Point in 1977). With them gone, it was a race he couldn't lose.\nSitting in midfield on the rail, he began a long run down the backstretch and was already level with the leaders before the third turn. Leading into the straight, he found a second wind and drew away, but Mejiro Phantom closed fast. Green Grass dug in and held him off by a nose - a run of pure grit, as if carrying TT's spirit.\nGreen Grass achieved the fairytale finish TT never had, bringing a glorious close to the TTG story.
