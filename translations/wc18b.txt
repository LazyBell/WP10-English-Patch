93=* Fated Rivals and a Third Assassin\nThe previous year's Horse of the Year was double classic winner Take Hope, while Haiseiko received a special "People's Award". In summer 1973, while both were resting, a horse returned from a 20-month layoff after surviving a life-threatening illness: Tanino Chikara. Half-brother to double classic winner Tanino Moutiers, he had been watched since before his debut but had just one win. Running with a unique low-headed style, he won race after race all the way to the Tenno Sho (Autumn). Just four months after his return, a "power crawling along the ground" had broken into the Big Two.\nHaiseiko and Take Hope, who had carried the racing boom, both announced the year-end Arima Kinen as their final race. Favorite was Take Hope, Tenno Sho (Spring) winner, who now finally topped Haiseiko in popularity too. Haiseiko, beaten in all 6 starts at 2400m or more, was 3rd choice, with Tanino Chikara 2nd. Kitano Kachidoki, winner of the Satsuki Sho and Kikuka Sho and that year's Horse of the Year, didn't run.\nTanino Chikara took the lead at the start. The year before he had watched Haiseiko too closely, couldn't catch the leaders and was 4th - this bold tactic was the lesson learned. Haiseiko closed from mid-race, Take Hope surged from behind, and it came down to three. In the straight Tanino Chikara pulled away from both and won by 5 lengths from Haiseiko. But all eyes were on the battle for 2nd and 3rd between Haiseiko and Take Hope, after 9 epic battles. Fans felt an era ending with the retirement of the two who had led the boom.\n\n* "Single-Bracket" Designation, Mark of a Favorite\nBetting then was mainly bracket quinella, and if a favorite was scratched, bettors could only hope for the outsider in the same bracket. Haiseiko's huge support in the previous year's Japanese Derby exposed the problem, and in 1974 "single-bracket designation" was introduced, placing a horse expected to draw heavy betting alone in its bracket. It was first used in that year's Satsuki Sho, where unbeaten Kitano Kachidoki was given a single bracket and won.
97=* Farewell, Ten Point\nThe year before, TTG had thrilled racing. Having finally beaten his nemesis Tosho Boy, who retired after the Arima Kinen, Ten Point set out this year on his long-planned trip abroad. But many Kansai fans wanted to see him once more before he left, so his camp entered him in the Nihon Keizai Shinshun Hai (now the Nikkei Shinshun Hai) at Kyoto. Everyone believed it would be a send-off win.\nIn falling snow, carrying a massive 66.5 kg, Ten Point led from the start down the backstretch. Erimo George and others pressed him, but just as everyone thought he was in a different class, he suddenly dropped back before the final turn. It was a severe fracture. Despite desperate treatment, 43 days later Ten Point passed away.\nHis tragic death was widely reported, bringing grief to racing and raising many issues, such as whether to assign excessively heavy weights. Two days after his death a farewell ceremony was held at Ritto Training Center - the first funeral ever held for a thoroughbred in Japan.\nFarewell, Ten Point... Shuji Terayama, a poet and playwright who loved racing, dedicated a poem to him, and many fans grieved.\n\n* Hatsushiba O, Toward an Unprecedented Fourth Crown\nSouthern Kanto's classics, the spring Haneda Hai and Tokyo Derby plus the Tokyo Okan Sho founded in autumn 1964, were modeled on the JRA's European-style classics (first two legs in spring, third in autumn).\nThis year Southern Kanto's star was Hatsushiba O, son of Takeshiba O, who ran on turf or dirt at any distance. He won the Haneda Hai and Tokyo Derby in spring, then the Tokyo Okan Sho by 6 lengths in autumn, the third Southern Kanto triple crown winner ever.\nBut he didn't stop. Aiming for a fourth crown that the two previous triple crown winners (Hikaru Takai and Golden Ribot) never managed, he ran in the year-end Tokyo Daishoten and won by two and a half lengths, the first Southern Kanto quadruple crown ever. The European-style format lasted until 1995, but only two others won all four (San Oi in 1983 and Rosita in 1989).\n\n* Miho Training Center Opens\nJapanese racing originally did everything at racecourses nationwide, from development and training to racing. But as racing's popularity and horse numbers grew, racecourse stables neared their limits, and urban sprawl around tracks made keeping horses there harder. So "training centers" dedicated to training and developing racehorses were planned.\nIn Kansai a site was found smoothly and Ritto opened in 1969, but in Kanto choosing and buying land dragged on. Finally, 17 years after it was planned, Miho opened in 1978. With both centers open, the East-West battle intensified and racing developed further.
