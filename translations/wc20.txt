105=* "Bewitching Dark Bay" Mejiro Ramonu\nIn the year free "racing programs" began being handed out so every visitor could enjoy racing more, a new heroine was born: Mejiro Ramonu, a filly with a beautiful dark bay coat.\nShe won her debut by 20 lengths, showing her talent, but her true power emerged the next year when she teamed up with jockey Hiroshi Kawachi. In the 1986 Yonsai Himba Tokubetsu (West) (now the Fillies' Revue), she dropped to last when the leaders faltered, then burst clear in the straight with an astonishing late charge. She went on to win the Oka Sho, the Yonsai Himba Tokubetsu (East) (now the Flora S.) and the Oaks for an easy double. Kawachi compared her to Tesco Gaby, famed as the greatest filly ever: "I think she's surpassed her."\nIn autumn she won the Rose S. and went for the Triple Crown in the Queen Elizabeth II Cup. Hopes for the first fillies' Triple Crown made her 1.3. She moved from the third turn and drew level with the leaders. Used to winning from behind, her early move looked premature to crowd and commentator alike, but she held off the rest in the straight to win by half a length - the first fillies' Triple Crown in JRA history. Having also won every trial, she was hailed as a "perfect Triple Crown." Several fillies have since won the Triple Crown, but she alone did it perfectly.\nWith no graded wins against colts, some rate her only "the best filly," but the next year her Triple Crown earned her a Hall of Fame place alongside seven-crown winner Symboli Rudolf - the first filly or mare inducted.\n\n* Japan's First Night Racing\nSouthern Kanto local racing runs mainly on weekdays to avoid clashing with weekend JRA racing. At first it ran on weekday afternoons, which salaried workers couldn't attend. So in 1986 Oi Racecourse began "Twinkle Races," night racing that people could attend after work - the first night racing not just in horse racing but in all public betting sports, including keirin. Night racing proved popular and is now held at many local tracks.
106=* Sakura Star O, Blooming in Autumn\nSakura Star O was born with part of a leg bent inward. He won his second start, but favoring the leg led to an injury, and he was out for 4 months.\nReturning in 1987, he ran down the field from 4th, widest of all, with a powerful finish in the Yayoi Sho. Little noticed as 6th choice, he suddenly became a classic contender. In the Satsuki Sho his astonishing finish won it by two and a half lengths, in the third-fastest time ever after Symboli Rudolf and Tosho Boy. But his legs couldn't take the speed, another injury needed 4 months to heal, and he missed the Derby.\nWith a return in sight, his camp set the Kikuka Sho as his first autumn race. Before the grueling 3000m race, a prep was standard, so this was highly unusual. But his camp felt strongly: "We couldn't even give him the Derby - at least let him run in the Kikuka Sho." Far from 100%, and doubted after six months off, the Satsuki Sho winner was only 9th choice. But he burst up the inside in the straight to win clearly. No horse had ever won the Kikuka Sho in his first autumn race; after 202 days off, it was the longest gap ever for a Kikuka Sho or any classic winner. The announcer's cry before the line - "Cherry blossoms in full bloom in chrysanthemum season!" - is still remembered as a classic call.\n\n* Improving Racing's Image\nAfter the first racing boom raised its social standing, some still saw racing as gambling and stayed away. The Japan Racing Association tried many ways to change that image. This year it adopted the abbreviation "JRA" (Japan Racing Association).\nIts off-track betting sites were also given the friendlier name "WINS," a double meaning of "WINning Spot" and "Weekend IN Spot."\nRacing's better public image today may be the result of such steady efforts.
107=* The "Common Sense" About Greys\n"Grey horses don't win" was racing's common sense. Even after grey father and son Mejiro Asama and Mejiro Titan, the belief remained deep-rooted.\nOne grey struggled from his debut: Tamamo Cross. But from autumn of his 3yo year, switched from dirt, where he had done nothing, to mainly turf, he showed his true worth. In 1988 he won the Tenno Sho (Spring), drawing attention as "the late-arriving star."\nAnother grey also emerged: Oguri Cap, who joined the JRA after dominating at local Kasamatsu. His power was overwhelming, and some likened him to Haiseiko, who also moved from local racing and sparked the first boom.\nThe two greys met in the Tenno Sho (Autumn). The clash drew such buzz that some fans camped overnight at Tokyo's gates. Tamamo Cross surprised everyone by racing forward, then held off Oguri Cap's desperate charge to win - Oguri Cap's first loss since joining the JRA. In their second meeting, the Japan Cup, both lost to foreign horse Pay the Butler, but Tamamo Cross was 2nd and Oguri Cap 3rd. Afterwards it was announced Tamamo Cross would retire after the Arima Kinen.\nThen came the final grey duel, the Arima Kinen. Determined to win, Oguri Cap's camp brought in master jockey Yukio Okabe. Late in the race Oguri Cap led in the straight, and Tamamo Cross drew level from last, widest of all. After a fierce duel Oguri Cap won by half a length, avenging two defeats - the first Arima Kinen win by a grey.\nAfter seeing this grey duel, called "the last great race of the Showa era," no one believed "grey horses don't win" anymore.\n\n* A Festival for Jockeys\nThis year the Golden Jockey Cup, an invitational jockeys' race at Sonoda Racecourse in Amagasaki, Hyogo, was founded. Only jockeys with 2,000 or more career wins, JRA or local, are invited to compete. Jockeys who usually wear their owners' silks ride in their own original colors - a treat for fans. It is still held today.\n\n* Kansai Strikes Back\nSince the hill training course opened at Ritto in 1985, Kansai horses had steadily improved. This year they finally passed Kanto horses in total wins - and Kansai horses have out-won Kanto horses every year since. It was the turning point when the East-West balance flipped.
108=* The Heisei Big Three\nThe year before, racing was thrilled by the grey duel. In this, the first year of Heisei, the epic battles of three horses sparked the second racing boom: Oguri Cap, Inari One and Super Creek, the "Heisei Big Three."\nInari One moved from local Oi. While Oguri Cap and previous Kikuka Sho winner Super Creek rested in the first half of the year, he won the Tenno Sho (Spring) and the Takarazuka Kinen, rising fast on the main path with fierce fighting spirit. In autumn he met Oguri Cap for the first time in the Mainichi Okan, a thriller decided by photo: Oguri Cap by a nose, showing the pride of "the idol horse of the age."\nIn the Tenno Sho (Autumn) Super Creek joined, and the future Heisei Big Three met for the first time. From the widest draw, thought a disadvantage, Super Creek raced forward with ease and held off Oguri Cap's charge by a neck.\nAll three met again in the Arima Kinen. Thanks to recent form, Oguri Cap and Super Creek got single brackets, and it was billed a "Big Two." As expected, the two raced forward and rounded the final turn together. But in the straight Inari One charged as if to prove he was one of the "Big Three," hitting the line level with Super Creek, and won by a nose after a photo.\nThe Heisei Big Three thrilled racing with neck-and-neck battles, and kept leading the boom with more fierce clashes the next year.\n\n* Causes of the Second Racing Boom\nThe bubble economy was of course a big factor. But unlike the first boom, driven by horses like Haiseiko, this one was also boosted by attention on jockeys. Most popular of all was Yutaka Take, who rode all three of the Heisei Big Three. At first he was known as "Kunihiko Take's son," after his father, a top jockey until a few years before. But breaking the rookie wins record and becoming the youngest classic winner ever, he quickly became the star of the boom. He is still riding at the top today as racing's "living legend."\nThis year also saw the birth of Turfy, the JRA mascot. Perhaps helped by such efforts, child fans who couldn't even bet - unheard of in the first boom - surged.\n\n* A First for Greys\nGreys were starring, but late bloomer Tamamo Cross and Oguri Cap, who wasn't nominated for the classics, never ran in the Japanese Derby.\nBut this year a new grey, Winner's Circle, emerged. 2nd in the Satsuki Sho, he became the first grey ever to win the Derby. It was also the first Derby win by a horse bred in Ibaraki, where few are foaled, which made big news.
109=* The Wonder Brought by "the Wind God"\nThe second racing boom, which began in the late 1980s, peaked this year.\nAfter wins like the Asahi Hai Sansai S. the year before, Ines Fujin was seen as the top classic contender. But he lost the Yayoi Sho to Mejiro Ryan and the Satsuki Sho to Hakutaisei. Some called for jockey Eiji Nakano, his rider since his debut, to be replaced, but trainer Shusuke Kato kept him on.\nThen came the Japanese Derby. In the middle of the boom, with fans excited by the clash of Mejiro Ryan, Hakutaisei and Ines Fujin, over 190,000 people - matching the population of Fuchu city then - packed Tokyo Racecourse. It far exceeded the first boom's record and still stands as the world record for racecourse attendance.\nAs the gates opened, Ines Fujin went to the front. He never slowed; Hakutaisei and others closed, but he pulled away again, kept his lead in the straight and won from the front. Having run a stunning time that broke the race record by a second, he could only walk slowly afterwards, returning to the stands alone, far behind the rest. Seeing the winner's calm return, the huge crowd chanted "Nakano! Nakano!" for the jockey's superb ride and the horse who answered it. Chanting a winner's name was unheard of then - a new culture was born. It may have been the moment racing was accepted as a sport.\nAfter the race Ines Fujin suffered a tendon injury. He tried to return but couldn't, leaving the turf as if he had burned out in the Derby.\n\n* Oguri Cap's Miracle Last Race\nOguri Cap led the second boom. In his first race this year, the Yasuda Kinen, he was ridden by Yutaka Take, another pillar of the boom. Two of racing's biggest stars together drew huge attention, and Oguri Cap won in course-record time.\nBut he was 2nd in the Takarazuka Kinen, then a poor 6th in the Tenno Sho (Autumn). Aiming to bounce back in the Japan Cup, he ran a dismal 11th, his first double-digit finish. People whispered "Oguri Cap is finished," and some fans, unable to bear seeing him lose, pleaded, "Please just let him retire."\nHis camp chose the Arima Kinen as his farewell. They asked Take to ride him again, the first time since the Yasuda Kinen, and say they'd have withdrawn him had he refused. Over 170,000 fans packed Nakayama to see Oguri Cap's last race, but he was only 4th choice, and few thought he could win.\nJust before the start, Take reportedly encouraged his partner: "Hey, you're Oguri Cap, remember." The pace was unexpectedly slow, and many horses fought their riders. But the experienced Oguri Cap and Take saved their energy. Moving up from the third turn, he took the lead in the straight and held off the rest on sheer guts. The miracle last race, defying all predictions, set the crowd roaring, and soon chants of "Oguri! Oguri!" rang out. The idol horse who sparked the boom left the stage with the perfect finale.
110=* From Emperor to King\nThe bubble economy that thrilled Japan ended this year. With Oguri Cap retired the year before, the boom seemed set to end with the downturn - but a new hero heated it up even more: Tokai Teio, son of seven-crown "Emperor" Symboli Rudolf, given the title "King."\nAt birth Tokai Teio was rated low for his slender frame. But once training began, his rare suppleness changed everyone's minds. Using that flexibility for a long, flowing stride, he won his first 4 starts without his jockey ever using the whip. By now everyone saw his sire in him.\nUnbeaten, he won the Satsuki Sho despite the widest draw. Jockey Takayuki Yasuda, following Yukio Okabe, who won the Triple Crown on Symboli Rudolf, raised one finger - "first crown." In the Japanese Derby he sat in a good spot and broke clear at the final turn to win, the first Kansai winner since Bamboo Atlas in 1982. Yasuda proudly raised two fingers.\nBut afterwards Tokai Teio was found to have a fracture needing 6 months to heal. The dream of a father-son unbeaten Triple Crown ended without even a chance to try.\n\n* Toward Clean Racing\nThis year both JRA and local racing introduced demotion rules. If a horse that finished ahead is judged to have interfered with another, it is placed just behind the horse it hampered. Europe and America had used such rules since the 1950s, so Japan now matched international rules.\nBefore that, interference meant disqualification. But more disqualifications meant fans holding back bets for fear of wasted tickets, hurting sales. So in practice disqualifications were rare, and most cases ended with penalties for jockeys. Rough riding increased, but the new rules improved things.\nThat spring, champion older horse Mejiro McQueen won the Tenno Sho, completing three generations of winners. He also finished first in the Tenno Sho (Autumn), but was judged to have cut inside right after the start and blocked several horses, including the last-placed one, and was demoted to last.\n\n* Quinella Betting Begins\nThis year the JRA began selling "umaren," quinella bets by horse number. With this the single-bracket system of 1974 was abolished. Since bracket quinella began in 1963 no new bet types had been added - only win, place and bracket quinella.\nThe same year the "PAT" system for betting from home by computer was introduced. As racing flourished, ways to bet and enjoy it gradually widened.
