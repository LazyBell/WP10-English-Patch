44=* British Filly Sun Princess\nSun Princess was born in Ireland and trained in Britain. True to her name she was proud, and as a youngster often acted up in training. She ran only once at 2, finishing 2nd.\nIn 1983 she opened in the Sir Charles Clore Memorial S., an Oaks trial, finishing 2nd to earn her place in the Oaks. The favorite was French-trained Alexandrie. Held up early, Sun Princess came wide and took the lead half a mile (about 800m) out. Into the straight she drew away with every stride and won by 12 lengths, the biggest margin in Oaks history (broken in 2021 by Deep Impact's daughter Snowfall, by 16 lengths). So dominant was she that racing people speculated she would have won the Derby against the colts.\nShe then ran in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. against colts and older horses, but could only finish 3rd. Back among fillies in the Yorkshire Oaks, she led from the start and stretched her lead to 4 lengths in the straight. The press wrote that she ran with "tremendous zest."\nRiding that momentum, she took on the colts again in the St. Leger. The going was bad, and French Derby winner Caerleon was withdrawn, so her own participation was in doubt until just before the race. But her camp stood firm and she ran. Again she hit the front early in the straight and won by three-quarters of a length. Winning the Oaks and the St. Leger, the Sun Princess claimed a fillies' double.\n\n* Legendary Jockey Lester Piggott\nL. Piggott was Britain's greatest jockey. Born into a family of jockeys - his father, grandfather and maternal grandfather all rode - he grew up around horses and debuted as a jockey at 12. He was hailed as a prodigy, and in 1954 he won his first Derby at 18 on Never Say Die. He kept winning, including the Triple Crown with Nijinsky, and became a true star. He was especially popular with housewives, and helped spread racing's appeal to the general public at a time when it was still seen as a pastime of the upper class.\nIn the 1983 Derby he rode Teenoso, winner of the Derby Trial S. After a thunderstorm the night before, the going was extremely heavy. Teenoso settled in a good position, struck the front early and won safely by 3 lengths. It was Piggott's 9th Derby win, breaking his own record, which still stands today.\nPiggott died in 2022 at 86. Champion jockey in Britain 11 times, with over 5,000 wins at home and abroad, he was a true legend.
45=* John Henry, US Horse of the Year Again\nJohn Henry was named after a legendary working-class folk hero. With a poor pedigree and plain looks he was rated low. Worse, he had a terrible temper - when offered as a yearling at auction, he had banged his head against a wall and his face was covered in blood. To fix his temperament he was gelded before he even raced.\nDebuting in 1977, he started well with 3 wins from 6, but then lost 10 straight from autumn into the next year. The turning point was a claiming race - a race where every runner is for sale. Japan has none, but in America, where racehorses are traded actively, they make up about half of all races. In one such race John Henry, who had always run on dirt, tried turf for the first time. He took the lead at the final turn, never let anyone near, and won by 14 lengths. No one claimed him, but his rise had begun. In 1980 he switched to turf, won a G3 and G2s three in a row, took his first G1 in the San Luis Rey S., and won 4 G1s that year. The next year he became the first champion of the Arlington Million, the world's first race worth $1 million. With 5 G1 wins that year he was named US Horse of the Year, the first unanimous choice.\nAt 7 and 8 he didn't get the results he wanted, but in 1984, at an amazing 9 years old, John Henry recaptured his peak form. In May he won the G3 Golden Gate H. in course-record time, the first graded-stakes win by a 9yo in US history. Through September he won 4 more G1s and was named US Horse of the Year for the second time, a fitting finale.\nJohn Henry won 16 G1s from 83 career starts, and lived to 32 - the grit of a horse who rose from the bottom.\n\n* The Breeders' Cup, a Festival of Racing, Is Born\nIn 1970s America, Triple Crown winners like Secretariat, Seattle Slew and Affirmed thrilled racing fans. But racing struggled to win new fans, and with lotteries and casinos being legalized, its popularity was fading. Worried breeders from across the US came together and created the Breeders' Cup in 1984. Seven races were held in one day, including the "Classic" for older horses at middle distances and the "Distaff" for fillies and mares 3 and up, each with huge prize money. Staked on the breeders' pride, the event was a huge success and is now a festival of racing that draws top horses from around the world.
46=* Oh So Sharp's British Fillies' Triple Crown\nOh So Sharp was a filly owned by Sheikh Mohammed, later ruler of Dubai. Born in Ireland and trained in Britain, her pedigree was packed with great European and American sires - a true blueblood. On debut she was favorite in a field of 21 and lived up to it, winning. She won 3 straight, including a graded stakes, to end her first year.\nIn 1985, after 7 months off, she started in the Nell Gwyn S., a 1000 Guineas prep, and won by a length and a half, heading for the 1000 Guineas two weeks later. In a field including Triptych, a French G1 winner the year before, Oh So Sharp was favorite at 3.0. Instead of her usual forward tactics, she raced in a good spot in midfield. With 100 yards (about 91m) to go she was about 2 lengths behind the two leaders, but she surged and just caught them on the line, winning by a short head (a margin used abroad, smaller than a head). The runner-up Al Bahathri was owned by Mohammed's brother Sheikh Hamdan, so Oh So Sharp beat her owner's brother's horse to give Sheikh Mohammed his first British classic.\nIn the Oaks she met Triptych again, the first filly to win the Irish 2000 Guineas, but Oh So Sharp was favorite at 2.5. On soft going she came wide on the best ground, breezed past Triptych and won by 6 lengths, one step from the fillies' Triple Crown.\nNext she took on older horses and colts in the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. Against a field including Japanese Derby winner Sirius Symboli, who had traveled all the way from Japan, she was 2nd to outsider Petoski at 13. After that first loss she was 2nd again in the Benson & Hedges Gold C., and went into the big one, the St. Leger, on two straight defeats. Hopes were high for the first fillies' Triple Crown winner since Meld in 1955, and she was favorite at 1.73. She outdueled stablemate Lanfranco, a two-time G1 winner, in a fierce fight and won - the first British fillies' Triple Crown winner in 30 years. As she was already past her peak, she then retired: favorite in all 9 of her races, 7 wins and two 2nds, a brilliant record. No filly has won the British fillies' Triple Crown since.\n\n* A Bitter Disqualification\nSagace was a French horse who debuted in 1983. In his 4th start he won the G3 Prix Niel, his first graded win, and from then on raced only at Longchamp. He was a well-beaten 11th in his first Arc, but won it the next year.\nIn 1985, riding that momentum, he won the Prix Ganay and Prix d'Ispahan with ease for 3 straight G1s. He won the Prix Foy prep and went for a second Arc. With Oh So Sharp and Derby winner Slip Anchor absent, Sagace was favorite. Running 2nd, he took the lead just before the straight. Rainbow Quest, 18th in the previous Arc, charged up beside him and it became a two-horse battle. After a fierce struggle Rainbow Quest weakened at the end and Sagace finished half a length ahead. But after the race P. Eddery, Rainbow Quest's jockey, protested furiously that Sagace had interfered with him. The protest was upheld, Sagace was placed 2nd, and the back-to-back Arc that had been within his grasp slipped away in a way no one expected.
47=* The Horse With the Highest Rating Ever\n1986 was the year Dancing Brave, called the best in Europe in the 80s, swept the racing world. With an astonishing finishing kick he won the 2000 Guineas, the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. and the Arc. His rating of 141 lbs in the International Classification, the highest ever, has never been beaten, making him arguably the greatest horse of all time, not just the 80s. (In 2013 past ratings were reviewed and his was cut to 138, but the change was later reversed.)\nThe only horse to beat Dancing Brave in Europe was Shahrastani. In the Derby he held off Dancing Brave's mighty finish by half a length. Dancing Brave came from a hopeless position and fell just short, and his ride drew criticism, with jockey Greville Starkey taking the brunt. Starkey had confidently said beforehand that the distance was no problem for a horse who had only ever run a mile, which fueled the criticism. On the other hand, the race showed Dancing Brave's power, closing his last furlong (200m) in an astonishing 10.3 seconds. Shahrastani proved he was no ordinary horse either, winning the Irish Derby by 8 lengths next time out.\nDancing Brave then won an Arc with what was called the best field ever, in record time of 2:27.7, cementing his place as the greatest.\nCuriously, Mill Reef, hailed as Europe's best 15 years earlier, died this very year, as if handing over to the new champion.\n\n* "Iron Ladies" of America and Europe\nMeanwhile in America, a mare was drawing attention with a North American record of 8 G1 wins in a year: Lady's Secret, arguably Secretariat's finest offspring. She won 8 G1s including the Whitney H. and the Breeders' Cup Distaff, and was a fine 2nd against colts in the Woodward S. She was only the second filly or mare named Horse of the Year since the Eclipse Awards began. For her toughness, running 15 times that year, she was called "The Iron Lady."\nEurope had its own tough "Iron Lady" at the same time: Triptych, who won 8 G1s against colts in 41 career starts. She drew attention as Dancing Brave's rival; this year she was 2nd in the Eclipse S. and 3rd behind Dancing Brave in both the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth Diamond S. and the Arc. She later traveled to the Breeders' Cup Classic in America and the Japan Cup in Japan, becoming known around the world.\nLady's Secret and Triptych never met, but two great mares both called "Iron Lady," famous at the same time in America and Europe, may be a curious twist of fate.
