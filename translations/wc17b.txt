87=* The Birth of the "Big Three"\nMany groups of rivals from the same crop have been called a "Big Three," but this year the first-ever "Big Three" rose in Japanese racing: Takeshiba O, Asaka O and Marchis.\nTakeshiba O was by China Rock, later a leading sire, but his dam never raced and his granddam was a maiden at local tracks - hardly a blueblood - and as a youngster he wasn't well built. He lost his first two starts, won his third, then won 6 straight from his fifth. In his first graded win, the Asahi Hai Sansai S., he won by a race-record 7 lengths and became the top classic contender.\nAsaka O, by contrast, was by Hindostan, the great sire of five-crown winner Shinzan, and closely related to British Derby winner Pinza - a blueblood called "the young prince."\nMarchis was the only one of the three from the Kansai region. His striking chestnut coat made him a fan favorite, and he was favorite in 21 of his 31 starts. He had the ability to match, winning the Hanshin Sansai S. to become the West's 2yo champion.\nThe three first met in the Yayoi Sho, a classic trial. Favorite was Takeshiba O, who had won his last race, the Tokyo Yonsai S. (moved to dirt due to snow), by 8 lengths in record time. He and the speedy 2nd choice Cadmus fought for the lead at a blistering pace. Takeshiba O got in front down the backstretch but ran out of steam and was caught by Asaka O before the line: Asaka O 1st, Takeshiba O 2nd, Marchis 3rd. Marchis then won the Spring S. With top contender Takeshiba O beaten twice, the race for the crown was now seen as a three-way battle.\nThe first classic, the Satsuki Sho, was run in the rain. Takeshiba O, who loved heavy ground, was favorite. He was slow away but took the lead before the third turn. In the straight he dueled with Asaka O, but the race went to Marchis, charging from the back. The top 3 were the same as in the Spring S.\nThe second leg, the Japanese Derby, was held a month late on July 7 due to stand renovations at Tokyo Racecourse, and was called the "Tanabata Derby." It also fell on the day of the House of Councillors election, so only 8 races were run instead of the usual card. Favorite was Marchis, who had won the Satsuki Sho and also beaten Takeshiba O in the NHK Hai, followed by Takeshiba O and Asaka O. When the race began, 9th choice Tanino Hello More, using gate 1, shocked everyone by going to the front. Normally Takeshiba O and Asaka O would follow, but perhaps wary of Marchis after the Satsuki Sho, they stayed stuck in midfield. In the straight Takeshiba O surged up the inside, threatening to take the lead, but it was too late. Tanino Hello More never weakened and led all the way to the line. Takeshiba O was 2nd, 5 lengths back, Asaka O 3rd and Marchis 4th - bitter for all three. Some called it luck, but Spartan training had made his talent bloom.\nFor the last classic, the Kikuka Sho, Takeshiba O had accepted an invitation to the Washington DC International in America, so it looked like a duel between Marchis and Asaka O. Tanino Hello More led again, but Asaka O, held up at the back, swept past him and Marchis in the straight to win his first classic. The Big Three's battle remained even, carried over to the next year.\n\n* Southern Kanto's Champion Shines in the JRA\nThe Southern Kanto local racing triple crown began in 1964. In 1967 Hikaru Takai became the first horse to win all three.\nThe next year he moved to the JRA, finished 2nd in all three open races, and went into the Tenno Sho (Spring) as favorite. On heavy ground he tracked the leaders, took the lead at the top of the straight and drew away to win by 2.8 seconds - officially "a distance," about 17 lengths. A rout like that was no fluke of the rain. He then won the Takarazuka Kinen in record time, proving to the racing world that his power was real.\n\n* Great Sire Hindostan Dies\nThis year the great sire Hindostan passed away. A British-bred who won the Irish Derby and more, he went to stud in Ireland but did little in five years there. The Hidaka Light Horse Promotion Association in Hokkaido spotted him, formed the first stallion syndicate in Japanese racing history, and imported him for 12 million yen, a huge sum then.\nIn 1961, his 6th year at stud in Japan, his offspring won an amazing 4 of the Big Eight races, and he became leading sire. He went on to sire five-crown winner Shinzan and more, winning 7 sire titles in all. His offspring's 117 graded wins still ranks near the top.\nHindostan died in 1968, while his son Asaka O was starring in the classics. His body was preserved and donated to the Japan Racing Association, and can still be seen at the horse museum in Urakawa, Hokkaido.
88=* Takeshiba O at His Peak\nThe year before, Takeshiba O was seen as the leader of the "original Big Three," but never won a single head-to-head with them. He opened this year 2nd again in the Nanakusa S., his 7th straight 2nd in Japan.\nBut from here his real worth emerged. He ran in the Tokyo Shimbun Hai on dirt, a surface he had raced on only twice, and won by 6 lengths in record time. In a dirt open race he won by a distance, again in record time, carrying 60 kg. That Japanese record for 1700m on dirt stood for 37 years.\nHe then won the Kyoto Kinen on turf and an open race carrying 60 kg or more. Finally, in the Tenno Sho (Spring), he faced Asaka O and Marchis in a Big Three showdown for the first time in a while. Takeshiba O, on a winning streak, was favorite, followed by Asaka O and Marchis. The pace was slow; Takeshiba O led in the straight and held off the charging Asaka O by 2 lengths - his first win against the Big Three, and his first Big Eight race.\nHe won twice more, including an open special carrying a career-high 65 kg, and became the first "100-million-yen horse," passing 100 million yen in career earnings.\nThat year he won 8 straight, 4 in record time. Dominant at any distance and on any surface, setting record after record, he was called "the monster" by fans. Later horses would be called "the grey monster" and "the shadow-roll monster," but Takeshiba O is said to be the "original monster," the first in racing history to bear the name.\n\n* A First Step Toward the Arc\nSpeed Symboli did little in the classics - 21st in the Satsuki Sho, 8th in the Japanese Derby - but ran 2nd in the Kikuka Sho, and the next year won 3 straight, including the Tenno Sho (Spring).\nHe kept running well and raced on this year at 7 (old age system). He was 3rd in his first race, then won the Meguro Kinen and the Diamond S. Seeing this, his camp decided on a trip to Europe, with the ultimate goal of the Arc - the first Japanese horse to try it. First he went to Britain for the King George VI & Queen Elizabeth S., but caught the equine flu then spreading in Britain, his preparation suffered, and he was 5th. He was 10th in the Grand Prix de Deauville prep and unplaced in the Arc itself - a crushing trip.\nHe recovered on a break and ran in the Arima Kinen. The field included Akane Tenryu, who had improved rapidly over summer to win the Kikuka Sho and was called "the greatest late bloomer since the war." The two dueled down the straight, and Speed Symboli won by a nose - the first 7yo ever to win the Arima Kinen.\nHis trip showed Japan's best were still far from world class, but his daring challenge was a huge achievement.\n\n* Ritto Training Center Opens\nRacehorses had always been trained at stables at each racecourse. But as racing boomed and the number of horses grew, the courses' stables could no longer cope, and dedicated "training centers" for managing and training racehorses were planned. This year Ritto Training Center finally opened, changing how racehorses were developed.
89=* The Epic Duel of Arrow Express vs Tanino Moutiers\nThe 1970 classics were thrilled by an East vs West clash.\nFrom the East came Arrow Express. The year before he won his debut in record time and the Asahi Hai Sansai S., the Kanto 2yo championship, breaking the 2yo record for 1600m on turf by 0.8 seconds. Unbeaten in 5, he was named champion 2yo colt.\nFrom the West came Tanino Moutiers. He lost some races in his first year, but won the Kyoto Sansai S. and the Hanshin Sansai S. in a row to become the undisputed Kansai 2yo champion.\nThey first met in the Spring S., a trial for the Satsuki Sho. Billed as the "A-T Showdown" after their initials, it drew a crowd of over 100,000 to Nakayama. 2nd choice Arrow Express went forward, with favorite Tanino Moutiers chasing. In the straight Arrow Express took the lead about 200m out, but hopes of a win were short-lived: Tanino Moutiers came wide with an astonishing finish and swept past him before the line. The margin was three-quarters of a length, but it was a clear win. In the Satsuki Sho, Tanino Moutiers won the duel from 200m out by a head. In the NHK Hai, their third meeting before the Derby, Tanino Moutiers was slow away and Arrow Express won by two and a half lengths.\nThen came the big one, the Japanese Derby. Perhaps because it was in Tokyo, Kanto horse Arrow Express was a heavy favorite with 41.8% of win bets, and Tanino Moutiers 2nd choice. But perhaps due to that morning's workout, Arrow Express was fretful and sweating in the paddock, while Tanino Moutiers was in perfect shape. As usual Arrow Express went forward, but struggled to get through the pack around the third and fourth turns and began to look in trouble. Tanino Moutiers glided forward wide, had the leaders in range at the final turn, and from about 200m out dueled with front-runner Date Tenryu, getting the better of him by half a length to complete the double. Arrow Express was 5th. Kansai horses took the top 3, a bitter result for Kanto.\nNeither won again before retiring. At stud, as if avenging his own defeat, Arrow Express's offspring did brilliantly, while Tanino Moutiers's did little. Arrow Express was leading sire in 1980 and 1981.\n\n* Masato Shibata's Bitter Replacement\nThis year Masato Shibata, a young jockey in his 3rd year, had ridden Arrow Express since his debut. But before the Satsuki Sho, he was told the owner wanted another jockey. Unable to accept it, Shibata asked the trainer why, and the trainer, in tears, told him: "Arrow is already Kanto's star. To beat Moutiers from Kansai, he needs Kanto's top jockey. If it hurts, then prove yourself better." Shibata turned that frustration into fuel, resolving to become a jockey who would never be taken off, and in 1988 he became Japan's champion jockey. He later said, "Because I couldn't ride Arrow, I became a real jockey."\n\n* Speed Symboli, Old Warrior\nSpeed Symboli had nearly retired, but raced on this year at 8 (old age system). His camp had heard the JRA might create an international invitational race, and were determined: "We can't stop without running Speed Symboli, who has fought the world." In the end the plan was shelved after breeders protested the decision to free up horse imports, and it was delayed over 10 years until the Japan Cup. But Speed Symboli paid no attention and ran superbly.\nIn his first race, the American JCC, he won in Japanese record time carrying 60 kg, and he won the Takarazuka Kinen in record time too, showing no signs of age. At year's end he ran in his 5th straight Arima Kinen. Favorite was Akane Tenryu, whom he had beaten by a nose the year before; Speed Symboli was 3rd choice behind autumn Tenno Sho winner Mejiro Asama. He boldly took the rough inside, led at the top of the straight, and though nearly spent, held off Akane Tenryu's fierce charge with grit to win by a neck again - the first horse to win the Arima Kinen twice. He retired after this race and was named Horse of the Year for the second time, three years after the first.
90=* The Little Filly Tomei's Challenge\nDescribed as "like a mouse," Tomei sold for just 1.65 million yen. She was small even for a filly, weighing under 400 kg even in her racing days. Naturally she was rated low, but after debuting in 1968 she turned her fiery temper into fighting spirit, winning 4 of 6 that year. The next year she didn't win a classic, but won races like the Kyoto Yonsai Tokubetsu. In 1970 she improved further, winning twice in a row including the Milers C. She was favorite for the Hankyu Hai but finished 2nd, then split a hoof and had to rest for a long time.\nThen came 1971. After over six months off, she returned in an open race. She didn't win at first, but after finally winning in her 4th start she seemed to awaken, reeling off 4 straight wins including the Milers C. and the Hankyu Hai. In autumn she tried her first long race, the Tenno Sho (Autumn). Her longest race had been the 2400m Japanese Oaks, and as a proven miler she was seen at a disadvantage, but she won anyway. For her final race she chose the Arima Kinen. With top horses out due to equine flu, Tomei was 2nd choice behind Tenno Sho winner Mejiro Musashi. She raced from last, took the lead at the final turn and held on to win clearly by a length and a half. It was the first Arima Kinen win by a filly or mare in 11 years, and she became the first filly or mare ever named Horse of the Year. None followed for 26 years, until Air Groove in 1997.\n\n* The Upstart's Brilliant Run\nIn racing, a horse whose pedigree is uncertain and not recognized as a thoroughbred is called a "thoroughbred-type." Hikaru Imai was one of them, born not on a racehorse farm but on a rice farm that bred horses on the side. His price was just 1.5 million yen - and he was bought for only 750,000 yen, on the promise that "the other half will be paid if he wins his debut." He won his debut as 5th choice, and the breeder received the promised 750,000 yen. He kept winning and ended his 2yo year with 3 wins and two 2nds from 5.\nIn 1971, at 3, his form was erratic, but he won his first graded race, the Kisaragi Sho. In the first classic, the Satsuki Sho, he was 4th choice, but he swept past the leaders in the straight to win. In the Japanese Derby he raced around 23rd, far behind the so-called "Derby position" (top 10 at the first turn), but again his superb finish swept him past the field for his second classic. He never won again, and retired after a tendon injury. Rising from thoroughbred-type to double classic winner before soon leaving the turf, the upstart captivated racing fans.
91=* The Kansai Big Three and the Shining Kick\nThe classic crop of 1972 (Showa 47) was packed with stars, one of the strongest generations in Japanese racing history, known as "the brilliant class of '47."\nThe classics were seen as a duel of the Kansai Big Three: Land Prince, Long Ace and Taiteem. The first classic, the Satsuki Sho, went to Land Prince, from the first crop of Tesco Boy. The Japanese Derby was pushed back to July by the flu, and also called the "Tanabata Derby." The Big Three fought it out, and favorite Long Ace won by a neck.\nThe final classic, the Kikuka Sho, was also seen as a Big Three battle, but 5th choice Kanto horse Ishino Hikaru, running for the second week in a row, ran down Taiteem to win. Suddenly the center of attention, he topped the fan vote for the Arima Kinen despite being only 3. That year's Arima Kinen drew three Tenno Sho winners - Mejiro Asama, Mejiro Musashi and Bell Wide - plus Big Three member Long Ace, and fans were thrilled; betting on the Arima Kinen passed 10 billion yen for the first time. Ishino Hikaru burst through wide with a superb finish to become the first 3yo colt to win the Arima Kinen. In the end Horse of the Year went not to the Kansai Big Three but to Ishino Hikaru.\nThe next year Taiteem, called "the uncrowned prince" after missing the classics, won the Takarazuka Kinen, and the year-end Arima Kinen went to Strong Eight of the same crop - "the brilliant class of '47" kept shining. Grand Marchis, who won the Nakayama Daishogai four times and became the first jumper inducted into the Hall of Fame, and Tanino Chikara, who overcame a serious injury to battle Haiseiko and Take Hope, were also of this crop.
92=* Idol Horse Haiseiko's Triumphant Run\nHaiseiko was a national idol who even appeared on the cover of a boys' manga magazine, and who sparked Japan's first racing boom. His power and popularity were truly monstrous.\nHe debuted at Oi, a local track, and won 6 straight, by a total of 56 lengths - unbeatable at Oi. Back then the wall between local and JRA racing was high, but Haiseiko moved to the JRA in style. As this "ronin" from local racing cut down the chosen elite one after another, the Japanese people saw themselves in him and went wild.\nHis JRA debut was the Yayoi Sho. Over 120,000 fans packed Nakayama, and fans at the front of the stands by the outer rail, trying to see him up close, were nearly crushed by the crowd surging from behind and fled onto the track. It wasn't the dazzling rout of his local days, but he won his first race for his new stable on turf for the first time, then won the Spring S. to stretch his streak to 8, and won the Satsuki Sho - the first ever by a horse from local racing.\nHe went into the Japanese Derby unbeaten with an incredible 66.7% of win bets. He tried to break clear from his usual forward spot, but couldn't quicken in the straight and was a shock 3rd. The Derby went to Take Hope - the horse Haiseiko would battle as his lifelong rival until retirement.\nThe Derby loss ended Haiseiko's myth of invincibility, but his popularity only grew - that is what makes him the original idol horse.\n\n* Take Hope Proves Himself With a Double\nTake Hope, meanwhile, was an unlucky horse, cast as the villain - "the Derby winner who beat the idol horse by a fluke." Angered at being called a fluke, his camp vowed to "beat Haiseiko!" in the Kikuka Sho.\nIn the Kikuka Sho, Haiseiko led 5 or 6 lengths clear at the final turn and kept going in the straight, but in mid-straight Take Hope charged and closed fast. The two pulled clear in a duel and hit the line together. The photo showed Take Hope had won by a nose. Beating Haiseiko "on ability, not popularity," Take Hope was finally accepted as Haiseiko's true rival.\nHe was named Horse of the Year (and champion 3yo colt), and the fact that he is the only colt besides Triple Crown winners to win both the Derby and the Kikuka Sho (he didn't run in the Satsuki Sho) proves his strength better than anything.\n\n* Betting Machines Arrive\nIn June this year, prototype betting machines began operating at Nakayama. Today buying tickets from machines at the track is normal, but back then people usually bought them at windows, handing over a slip with their selections or telling the clerk out loud. At some tracks there were even separate windows for each bet type, such as win and place.\nBeing able to bet more easily may have been one reason the racing boom caught fire.
